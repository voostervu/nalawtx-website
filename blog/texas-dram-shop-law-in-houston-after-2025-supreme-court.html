What the Texas Supreme Court's 2025 Dram Shop Ruling Means If You Were Hit by a Drunk Driver in Houston

In April 2025, the Texas Supreme Court made it harder to hold bars and restaurants liable when their customers cause DWI crashes. Here's exactly what changed, and what evidence now makes or breaks a dram shop claim in Houston.

You're sitting at a red light on Westheimer. A driver runs a stop sign and T-bones your car. Police arrive. The driver fails a field sobriety test. blood alcohol content of 0.17, more than twice the legal limit. Later you find out he'd been drinking for three hours at a bar three blocks away before he got behind the wheel.

You have a clear injury claim against the drunk driver. But you may also have a claim against the bar that kept serving him. That second claim, against the alcohol provider, is what Texas law calls a dram shop claim. And as of April 2025, winning one just got harder.

What Is Texas Dram Shop Law?

Texas's dram shop statute is found in the Texas Alcoholic Beverage Code § 2.02. It allows an injured person to sue a bar, restaurant, or other alcohol provider if two conditions are met: the provider served alcohol to someone who was obviously intoxicated at the time of service, and that intoxication was a direct cause of the person's injuries.

The phrase "obviously intoxicated" is the hinge the entire law turns on. Texas doesn't make every alcohol seller responsible for every drunk driver. The law only kicks in when a server or bartender had enough visible, observable evidence in front of them that a reasonable person would have known this customer was drunk, and they kept pouring anyway.

For years, plaintiffs could use a post-crash blood alcohol reading as one piece of that puzzle. A BAC of 0.17 at the time of the crash, combined with a three-hour bar tab and witness accounts of slurred speech, could support a jury's finding that the person was obviously intoxicated when the bar last served them. The April 2025 ruling in Roager v. Myers changed how that evidence works.

What Roager v. Myers Actually Decided

The Texas Supreme Court's decision in Roager v. Myers drew a sharp line between two kinds of evidence: what a server could have observed at the moment of service, and what investigators measured after a crash.

The court held that a post-crash BAC reading alone, even a high one, is not sufficient direct evidence of obvious intoxication at the time of service. The reasoning: alcohol metabolizes, time passes between last service and a crash, and a BAC number doesn't tell you what a bartender saw or should have seen when they handed over that last drink.

A breathalyzer number from a hospital or roadside test tells you what was in a person's blood after the crash. It doesn't prove what was visible to a server at the bar an hour earlier. The court is requiring plaintiffs to bridge that gap with direct, contemporaneous evidence.

In plain terms: you need eyewitness evidence from someone who was actually at the bar. Bar staff testimony, surveillance footage, receipts showing how many drinks were served over what period, other customers who saw the driver's condition before he left. That's the kind of evidence the court is looking for now.

This is a significant shift. Before this ruling, plaintiffs could sometimes lean heavily on a high BAC number and work backward, arguing that someone with a 0.17 BAC must have been showing signs of intoxication when last served. Post-Roager, that inferential bridge requires more support from direct observation evidence.

What This Means for DWI Crash Victims in Houston

Harris County sees thousands of DWI-related crashes every year. Houston's bar and restaurant corridors, Washington Avenue, Midtown, the East End, the Galleria area, generate a lot of late-night traffic that ends in crashes on I-10, Loop 610, and city streets. Many of those crashes involve drivers who were over-served.

The Roager ruling doesn't eliminate dram shop claims. It raises the evidentiary bar. Here's what that means practically:

Evidence preservation becomes urgent

Bars and restaurants routinely overwrite surveillance footage on 30- to 72-hour cycles. If you or your attorney don't send a preservation letter to the establishment within days of the crash, that footage may be gone permanently. In the post-Roager world, that footage is often the most important evidence you have.

The same urgency applies to receipts, point-of-sale records, and employee shift logs. These records can show exactly which server handled the table, how many drinks were ordered, and over what time period. Bars don't keep these indefinitely.

Witness identification matters more now

Other patrons at the bar who observed the drunk driver's behavior before he left, slurred speech, unsteady walking, knocking things over, are now critical witnesses. Their accounts can supply the "observable at time of service" evidence the court is demanding. Those witnesses scatter quickly. Getting their contact information fast is essential.

The drunk driver's claim remains separate and unaffected

The Roager ruling affects only the dram shop portion of your case, the claim against the alcohol provider. Your personal injury claim against the drunk driver himself is completely separate. That driver's liability for hitting you is governed by standard negligence law, and a high BAC reading is highly relevant there. The two claims run parallel to each other.

The "Obviously Intoxicated" Standard. What Courts Have Looked For

Even before Roager, Texas courts had been examining what "obviously intoxicated" actually looks like in the real world. The following types of evidence have historically supported a finding of obvious intoxication:

  • Server or bartender testimony that the customer was slurring words, stumbling, or behaving erratically
  • Bar surveillance video showing the customer's physical condition
  • Receipts or drink logs showing a pattern of heavy service over a short period
  • Other customers or employees who witnessed the person's condition
  • Evidence that the customer had been cut off or that staff discussed their level of intoxication

Post-Roager, this list didn't change. The ruling just confirmed that a post-crash BAC number can't substitute for contemporaneous observation evidence. Think of it as the court saying: show us what the bar staff saw. Don't just show us what the breathalyzer read two hours later.

What About the Drunk Driver's Own Responsibility?

Texas uses a system called modified comparative fault. Texas reduces what you can recover by your share of blame for the accident. In a DWI crash where you did nothing wrong, your percentage of fault is zero, and this rule doesn't hurt you at all.

In a dram shop case, comparative fault can get complicated because the court has to split responsibility among multiple parties: the drunk driver, the alcohol provider, and potentially others. That's one more reason these cases need experienced handling. The apportionment fight between the driver and the bar, each trying to shift more blame to the other, is real, and how it lands affects what you recover from each party.

Texas's Dram Shop Safe Harbor. One More Wrinkle

The Texas Alcoholic Beverage Code § 2.06 includes a provision sometimes called the "safe harbor" defense. An alcohol provider can use it to limit or avoid liability if they can show they required their employees to complete an approved seller-server training program, the employee actually completed it, and the employer didn't directly encourage over-service.

In practice, many Houston bars and restaurants will raise this defense immediately. That's why your attorney needs to look at not just what happened on the night of the crash, but how that establishment trained its staff, whether the program actually qualifies under Texas law, and whether there's evidence the employer's own culture pushed servers to keep drinks flowing regardless of a customer's condition.

A laminated "TABC certified" certificate on the wall doesn't automatically protect a bar. The safe harbor defense has conditions. If an employer looked the other way while staff over-served, that defense has real weaknesses.

The Two-Year Clock. Don't Let It Run Out

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, the statute of limitations for personal injury claims in Texas is generally two years from the date of the injury. This applies to both the claim against the drunk driver and the dram shop claim against the alcohol provider.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't. The evidence you need most, surveillance footage, server recollections, contemporaneous bar records, degrades or disappears in the first few days and weeks. By the time you're approaching the two-year mark, an attorney is left trying to build an "obviously intoxicated" case with almost nothing to work with.

If you were hit by a drunk driver anywhere in Harris County, at the intersection of Shepherd and San Felipe, on I-45 near downtown, or anywhere else in the Houston metro, and you believe alcohol was involved, the time to start preserving evidence is now.

What You Should Do Right Now If This Happened to You

You don't need to figure out whether a dram shop claim applies to your situation today. That's an attorney's job. But there are steps that protect your options:

  1. Get the police report. It will identify the establishment where the driver was drinking if that information came out at the scene.
  2. Note the name of the bar or restaurant if you learn it, from the police, the driver, witnesses, or social media.
  3. Don't contact the bar yourself. You don't want to tip them off that a claim is coming before a preservation letter goes out.
  4. See a doctor. Not just for your health. Your medical records document your injuries and create a timeline that matters in court.
  5. Talk to an attorney fast. The evidence window in dram shop cases is genuinely short. A preservation letter sent on day three is far more valuable than one sent on day thirty.

If the drunk driver had liability insurance, that insurer's adjuster will contact you quickly. They are not on your side. If there's also a dram shop angle to your case, you want someone in your corner before you say anything that could be used to undercut that claim.
