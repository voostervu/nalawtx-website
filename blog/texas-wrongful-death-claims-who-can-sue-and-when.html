Texas Wrongful Death Claims: Who Can Sue, What You Can Recover, and the Deadline You Cannot Miss

If someone you love was killed by another person's negligence, Texas law gives your family the right to seek compensation. But that right has strict limits on who can file and how long you have to act.

Losing a family member to a preventable accident is devastating. Then come the bills, the calls from insurance adjusters, the funeral arrangements. Somewhere in the middle of all that grief, you start wondering whether your family has any legal recourse.

Texas wrongful death law exists for exactly this situation. It holds the people responsible for your loved one's death financially accountable. But it only works if you file the right claim, with the right people, before the clock runs out. This post walks you through how that works in plain language.

What Is a Wrongful Death Claim in Texas?

A wrongful death claim is a civil lawsuit, separate from any criminal case, that your family files against whoever caused your loved one's death. You're not seeking to send anyone to prison. You're seeking compensation for what your family lost.

Texas created this legal right in the Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.001, 71.012. The law says that when a person dies because of another party's "wrongful act, neglect, carelessness, unskillfulness, or default," surviving family members can sue.

That covers a wide range of situations: an 18-wheeler driver who ran a red light on I-10, a drunk driver who crossed the centerline on Highway 290, a negligent doctor, a dangerous property owner, a defective product manufacturer. The common thread is that someone else's failure, not an unavoidable accident, took your family member's life.

A wrongful death claim is not about revenge. It's about making sure your family doesn't bear the financial consequences of someone else's negligence alone.

Who Has the Right to File Under Texas Law?

Texas law is specific about who can bring a wrongful death claim. Not every family member qualifies. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004, only three categories of people have standing to sue:

  • Surviving spouse. legally married at the time of death
  • Children. biological or legally adopted; adult children qualify, not just minors
  • Parents. biological or adoptive parents of the deceased

Siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles, and other relatives do not have standing to file a wrongful death claim in Texas, no matter how close the relationship was. This surprises a lot of families.

The eligible family members can file together in one lawsuit, or they can file separately. If none of them files within three months of the death, the estate's executor or administrator can file on the family's behalf. But the surviving family members can block that by objecting.

What about unmarried partners?

Common-law marriage is recognized in Texas. If you and your partner lived together, agreed to be married, and held yourselves out publicly as a married couple, you may qualify as a surviving spouse even without a formal ceremony. This is fact-specific and worth discussing with an attorney quickly. The clock is already running.

What if the deceased was a child?

Parents can file a wrongful death claim when a child is killed. Texas law also allows a separate survival action (discussed below) that can be filed on behalf of the child's estate for pain and suffering the child experienced before death.

The 2-Year Deadline. and Why It's Absolute

Texas gives families two years from the date of death to file a wrongful death lawsuit. This is the statute of limitations under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003.

Miss that deadline and you almost certainly lose your right to sue. It doesn't matter how strong your case is or how sympathetic your circumstances are. Courts routinely dismiss cases filed even one day late.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't. Here's why families run out of time:

  • Grief makes it hard to think about legal action in the first weeks and months
  • Investigations, especially in commercial truck crashes, take time to complete
  • Families sometimes wait to see if the insurance company will "do the right thing" voluntarily (they rarely do)
  • Finding the right attorney and building a case takes time

Starting early also matters for evidence. Truck companies are required to preserve driver logs, GPS data, and dash cam footage, but only if they receive a legal hold notice promptly. Waiting months can mean critical evidence is overwritten or destroyed.

The two-year clock starts on the date of death, not the date of the accident if they're different. An attorney can help you identify the exact date that governs your case.

Are there exceptions to the 2-year rule?

A few narrow situations can pause or extend the clock. If a government entity (like TxDOT or the City of Houston) is involved, you actually face a shorter deadline: a formal notice of claim must typically be filed within six months. If the defendant fraudulently concealed their role in the death, the clock may be extended. For minor beneficiaries, tolling rules may apply.

These exceptions are narrow and fact-specific. Don't assume they apply to your situation without talking to an attorney.

What Damages Can Your Family Recover?

Texas wrongful death law lets surviving family members seek compensation for what they personally lost because of the death. These are called beneficiary damages. They're different from the deceased person's own losses, which fall under a separate survival action.

Beneficiary damages in a Texas wrongful death claim

  • Loss of financial support. the income, benefits, and financial contributions your family member would have provided over their lifetime
  • Loss of companionship and society. the love, comfort, companionship, and guidance the deceased provided; available to both spouses and children
  • Loss of parental care and guidance. for children who lost a parent, the nurturing, advice, and emotional support they will no longer receive
  • Mental anguish. the grief, sorrow, and mental suffering caused by the loss
  • Loss of household services. the practical value of work the deceased performed around the home
  • Medical expenses. costs incurred for care of the deceased between the accident and the death
  • Funeral and burial expenses

Loss of companionship is one of the most significant, and most misunderstood, categories. It's not just about missing someone emotionally. Texas law recognizes that a spouse, a parent, a child provides real, tangible value to the people who loved them. That value can be presented to a jury.

The survival action. a separate but related claim

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.021, the deceased person's estate can also bring what's called a survival action. This claim captures what the deceased person experienced and lost before death: the pain and suffering they went through, any lost wages between the accident and their death, and property damage.

A survival action is filed by the estate's legal representative, not directly by the family members. In many cases, a wrongful death claim and a survival action are filed together. An attorney can help your family understand how both apply to your situation.

How Fault Works in Texas. and Why It Matters

Texas uses a system called modified comparative fault. If your loved one was partially at fault for the accident that killed them, the damages your family can recover are reduced by that percentage of fault.

If a jury finds the deceased was 20% at fault, the family's total recovery is reduced by 20%. There's a hard cutoff: if the deceased is found more than 50% responsible, the family recovers nothing.

This is exactly why insurance companies spend so much time after a fatal crash trying to build a narrative that blames the victim. They're not doing it out of curiosity. They're doing it to reduce or eliminate what they have to pay.

An attorney's job, in part, is to gather the evidence that tells the accurate version of what happened: crash reconstruction reports, witness statements, black box data from commercial trucks, surveillance footage. Along I-45, US-59, and the Beltway, where a disproportionate number of Houston's fatal crashes occur, that evidence often exists and it can be obtained if you act quickly.

When the Defendant Is a Company

Many Texas wrongful death cases don't just involve an individual driver. They involve corporations: trucking companies, employers, property owners, product manufacturers. This matters for several reasons.

Companies have professional claims teams and insurance carriers whose job is to limit payouts from day one. They will have investigators at the scene before most families even know what happened. They will pull electronic logging device data and internal records, and in some cases they will work to keep that data from ever reaching you.

Cases involving commercial 18-wheelers are a good example. Under federal regulations, trucking companies must maintain driver qualification files, hours-of-service logs, vehicle inspection records, and more. All of it is potentially relevant to a wrongful death claim. Getting a legal hold notice to the company immediately, before routine data purges happen, is critical.

Texas has seen several high-profile wrongful death suits involving commercial fleets in recent years. Families who waited to act faced real obstacles. Families who moved quickly had the evidence they needed.

What Happens If There's Also a Criminal Case?

Sometimes the person who caused the death is criminally charged: DWI manslaughter, criminally negligent homicide, vehicular manslaughter. A civil wrongful death claim is completely separate from that criminal case.

You don't need a criminal conviction to win a civil wrongful death case. The standard of proof is different. In a criminal case, the prosecution must prove guilt "beyond a reasonable doubt." In a civil case, the standard is "preponderance of the evidence," basically, more likely than not.

A criminal acquittal does not bar your family from pursuing civil claims. The two tracks run independently. Talk to a civil attorney regardless of what is happening on the criminal side.

Practical Steps for Families Right Now

If you've recently lost a family member and believe someone else's negligence was involved, here's what matters most in the near term.

Preserve everything you have. Photographs from the scene, the official accident report from HPD or the county sheriff's department, any communications from insurance adjusters, medical records, and anything the deceased shared about the incident before they died.

Do not give recorded statements to insurance adjusters. Adjusters for the at-fault party are not on your side. Anything you say can be used to assign blame to your loved one and reduce what you can recover. You are not required to give them a statement.

Talk to a Texas wrongful death lawyer before you sign anything. Releases, settlement offers, and authorization forms from insurance companies can permanently affect your family's rights. Once you sign, it's very difficult to undo.

Don't wait to see if the insurance company will offer something fair. They may make an early offer. Early offers are almost always low, sometimes dramatically so. They know your family is grieving and may not know the full value of what you've lost. An attorney can evaluate whether any offer is reasonable.
