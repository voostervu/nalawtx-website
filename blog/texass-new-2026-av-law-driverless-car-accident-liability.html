Texas's New 2026 Autonomous Vehicle Law: What Happens When a Driverless Car Injures You in Houston?

A new Texas law took effect May 28, 2026, putting the TxDMV in charge of authorizing driverless vehicles statewide and reshaping who you can hold responsible when one of those vehicles causes a crash.

Imagine you're stopped at the light on Westheimer at Beltway 8. A delivery van with no driver behind the wheel rolls through the intersection and T-bones your car. You're hurt. The van belongs to a tech company. There's no human driver to point to. Who do you call? Who pays your medical bills?

That scenario is no longer hypothetical in Houston. Texas leads the nation in commercial autonomous vehicle miles traveled, and driverless vehicles are already running on Houston-area roads: freight corridors along I-10, package deliveries in the Heights, passenger shuttles near the Texas Medical Center. With the new 2026 AV law now in effect, the rules around liability for these crashes just changed. Here's what you need to know if you're the one who got hurt.

What the 2026 Texas AV Law Actually Does

Before May 28, 2026, Texas had a patchwork approach to autonomous vehicles. The Texas Transportation Code permitted AVs on public roads under certain conditions, but no single agency had clear authority to authorize or regulate commercial driverless operations statewide.

The 2026 law changes that. It gives the Texas Department of Motor Vehicles (TxDMV) explicit authority to issue operating authorizations for fully autonomous vehicles. Companies that want to run driverless vehicles commercially in Texas now have to apply, demonstrate safety compliance, and maintain authorization from TxDMV. Lose that authorization and they can't legally operate.

That regulatory structure matters for injury victims. When a company holds a state authorization to run a driverless vehicle on public roads, it has accepted a formal set of responsibilities. That authorization paper trail becomes important evidence in any crash investigation.

Who Is Legally Responsible When an AV Hits You?

This is the question most injured people ask first. It's also the most complicated one, because there's rarely a single clean answer.

In a traditional car accident, you identify the negligent driver. With an autonomous vehicle, the "driver" is software, and behind that software is a web of potential defendants. Texas courts and injury attorneys are already working through these questions. Several parties can be on the hook depending on what caused the crash.

The AV company (the "operator")

Under Texas law, the entity that deploys the AV (the company that holds the TxDMV authorization) is treated as the operator. Texas Transportation Code provisions address this directly: when no human is in the driver's seat, the company operating the automated driving system steps into the legal role that a driver would otherwise occupy. That means the company can be liable for crashes caused by the system's failures the same way a negligent driver would be liable for theirs.

This is a significant shift from older vehicle accident law. The company can't simply point to the software and walk away.

The vehicle manufacturer

If the crash happened because of a defect in the vehicle itself. faulty sensors, brake failure, a hardware problem. the manufacturer may face a product liability claim. Product liability in Texas doesn't require proving the manufacturer was careless. You have to show the product was defective and that the defect caused your injury. Under Texas law, that can include design defects, manufacturing defects, or failure to warn.

The software developer

AV technology sometimes involves separate companies: one that makes the vehicle, another that writes the autonomous driving software. If the crash traces back to a software decision (the system misread a stop sign, failed to detect a pedestrian, or made a faulty lane-change calculation), the software developer could be a separate defendant.

A human driver in the mix

Many crashes don't involve a fully autonomous vehicle operating alone. Semi-autonomous systems, where a human is still required to monitor and take over, create a murkier situation. If the human driver failed to intervene when the system gave a warning, that driver's negligence re-enters the picture. Texas uses modified comparative fault: your recovery is reduced by your share of the blame, and you're barred from recovering anything if you're more than 50% at fault. In crashes involving partial automation, sorting out who was responsible for what, and when, requires detailed data analysis.

When an AV is involved, there are more potential defendants. More defendants means more insurance policies, more corporate lawyers, and more complexity. The flip side: more avenues to pursue full compensation for your injuries.

The Data Black Box: Your Best Evidence

Autonomous vehicles generate an enormous amount of data. Every second the system is running, it's recording: sensor readings, speed, steering inputs, system alerts, camera footage, the state of every software module. That data is the equivalent of a dashcam, an event data recorder, and an expert witness rolled into one, captured automatically on every trip.

That data is also in the possession of the company that hurt you. They have lawyers. They know what the data shows.

One of the most critical steps after an AV crash is sending what's called a litigation hold letter. It's a formal legal demand that the company preserve all data related to the incident. Without it, companies can argue that data was deleted through routine processes. With it, destroying evidence after notice exposes them to serious legal consequences.

This isn't something to try to do yourself. An attorney needs to send that letter fast, sometimes within days of the crash, before data retention cycles wipe the records.

Houston Is Ground Zero for This New Reality

Texas isn't just experimenting with autonomous vehicles. It's one of the leading commercial AV markets in the country. Houston's flat terrain, mild winters, and dense freight corridors along I-45, I-10, and US-290 make it attractive for AV operators. Autonomous long-haul trucks running between Houston and Dallas have been in commercial operation for years. Driverless delivery vehicles have been spotted in Midtown, Montrose, and near Hobby Airport.

Harris County roads are also some of the most dangerous in the state. When you combine high AV activity with heavy traffic and aggressive driving conditions, crashes involving autonomous systems are going to happen. In Houston, they already have.

The Harris County District Clerk's office and the Southern District of Texas federal court in Houston will likely see the first wave of significant AV injury litigation in this state. The legal frameworks are still forming. That cuts both ways: the law is unsettled, which creates uncertainty, but it also means there are no unfavorable precedents locked in yet for injured plaintiffs.

What to Do Right After an AV Crash

The steps after an autonomous vehicle crash are similar to any serious accident, with a few additions specific to AV cases.

Call 911 and get medical attention. Your health comes first. If you're injured, go by ambulance to Ben Taub, Memorial Hermann, or the nearest trauma center. Don't refuse treatment at the scene because you feel okay. AV crash investigations are complex, and your documented medical records are the foundation of any future claim.

Document everything at the scene. Photograph the vehicle. Get photos of its markings, company name, any visible sensor hardware (cameras, LIDAR units on the roof or bumpers), and license plates. If there's an operator or safety monitor nearby, get their name and employer. Note the exact location, time, and direction the vehicle was traveling.

Get the police report. Officers responding to the crash in Houston will file a report with HPD or the Harris County Sheriff's Office. That report should identify the vehicle as autonomous and note the operating company. If the officer doesn't flag it as an AV, that's something your attorney can address later.

Don't talk to the company's representatives. AV companies often deploy incident response teams quickly after crashes. They may approach you at the scene or call within hours. They are not there to help you. Refer them to your attorney.

Contact an attorney fast. The data preservation issue alone makes speed critical. Beyond that, Texas's statute of limitations for personal injury claims is generally two years from the date of injury under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Two years sounds like a long time until you're dealing with an injury, medical treatment, and a large corporation's legal team all at once.

The two-year clock is real. But the data preservation window is even shorter. If an AV hit you, days matter. Not just months.

What the Insurance Picture Looks Like

Under the 2026 Texas AV law, companies seeking TxDMV authorization must demonstrate financial responsibility, meaning they need to carry insurance or meet self-insurance requirements. The specific minimums are set through the authorization process, and commercial AV operators are typically required to carry substantially higher limits than a standard personal auto policy.

That sounds like good news, and it is, in theory. In practice, large corporations with large policies also have large legal departments whose job is to minimize what gets paid out. Expect the same insurance tactics you'd see in any serious crash: early contact, requests for recorded statements, lowball initial offers, and delays. The scale is just bigger.

One important difference from a standard car crash: there's no individual driver whose personal auto insurance is the primary policy. The corporate insurer steps in directly. Negotiations happen at a corporate level from day one, which is another reason having an attorney early changes the dynamic significantly. What About Semi-Autonomous Vehicles?

Not every AV crash involves a fully driverless vehicle. Many vehicles on Houston roads right now have partial automation: Tesla's Autopilot, GM's Super Cruise, Ford's BlueCruise, and similar systems. These systems assist the driver but still require human supervision. They are not covered the same way under the 2026 law, which focuses on vehicles operating without any human driver required.

If a semi-autonomous system was engaged when a crash happened, the liability analysis gets more layered. The human driver behind the wheel still has legal responsibilities. But if the system behaved in a way that contributed to the crash (sudden unexpected braking, phantom steering correction, failure to detect a vehicle in the lane), the manufacturer's product liability exposure comes into play alongside the driver's negligence.

These cases require pulling the vehicle's onboard data, often through a subpoena or formal discovery process, to establish what the system was doing at the moment of impact. That doesn't happen automatically. It requires legal action to compel.

The Bigger Picture: Texas Is Making the Rules in Real Time

Texas made a deliberate choice to be a permissive state for autonomous vehicles. The 2026 law is part of a longer pattern: Texas was one of the first states to allow AV testing without a human safety driver, and state leadership has consistently signaled that it wants AV companies operating here.

That's not inherently bad. The technology has real safety potential, and much of the innovation in this space is happening because Texas gave it room to develop. But permissive regulation also means the full consequences of accidents involving these systems are still being sorted out in courts and legislatures.

For injured people, the honest answer is: AV accident cases are harder than standard car accident cases. There are more defendants, more data, more lawyers on the other side, and less settled precedent to guide expectations. That doesn't mean they're unwinnable. It means they require attorneys who understand both personal injury law and the specific technical and regulatory layers these cases involve.

If you've been hurt by an autonomous or semi-autonomous vehicle anywhere in the Houston area, whether on Loop 610, in the Energy Corridor, in Katy, or on the Ship Channel freight corridors, the same fundamental principle applies as in any injury case: the company whose vehicle hurt you should be accountable for the harm they caused. The 2026 law creates a clearer framework for establishing that accountability. Use it.
