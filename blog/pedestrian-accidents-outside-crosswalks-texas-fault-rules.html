You Were Hit by a Car in Houston. But You Weren't in a Crosswalk. Can You Still Recover?

Texas's comparative fault rules don't automatically bar your claim just because you crossed mid-block. Here's what actually happens to your recovery, and how insurers use the "no crosswalk" argument to pay you less than you deserve.

A driver hit you. You're dealing with broken bones, hospital bills, and time off work. Then the insurance adjuster calls and says something like: "Our investigation shows you weren't in a marked crosswalk, so we believe you were at fault." They might say it politely. But the message is clear. They think you owe them a favor for answering the phone.

That's not how Texas law works. Being outside a crosswalk when you were struck doesn't automatically make the accident your fault. It doesn't automatically reduce your recovery to zero. But it does create a legal wrinkle you need to understand. If you don't, the insurance company is counting on you to accept far less than you're owed.

Houston's Pedestrian Crisis Is Real. and Getting Worse

Houston is one of the most dangerous cities in America for people on foot. In 2026 alone, fatal pedestrian crashes have been reported along Westheimer Road, I-45, Highway 6, and Highway 288. These aren't isolated incidents. They're the product of high-speed roadways, inadequate pedestrian infrastructure, and drivers who are distracted, impaired, or simply moving too fast for conditions.

Harris County consistently ranks among the deadliest counties in Texas for pedestrian fatalities. Many of those deaths, and countless serious injuries, happen away from marked crosswalks. That's not surprising in a city built around cars. Marked crosswalks can be blocks apart. Sidewalks disappear mid-street. Crossing at the nearest intersection isn't always physically possible.

When someone gets hit under those conditions, the driver's insurance company often leads with one argument: you shouldn't have been there. Understanding why that argument is legally weaker than they make it sound is the first step to protecting your claim.

What Texas Law Actually Says About Pedestrians and Crosswalks

Under the Texas Transportation Code, pedestrians do have certain obligations. When you cross at a location that isn't a marked or unmarked intersection crosswalk, you're required to yield the right of way to vehicles. That's real, and it matters to your case.

But here's what the adjuster won't tell you: drivers have obligations too. Texas law requires drivers to exercise due care to avoid colliding with a pedestrian, at all times, in all locations. That duty doesn't disappear just because a pedestrian is mid-block. A driver who's speeding, texting, drunk, or simply not paying attention can still be legally responsible for hitting someone who was crossing outside a crosswalk.

A pedestrian's location, crosswalk or not, affects the fault calculation. It rarely tells the whole story about who caused the crash.

Texas also recognizes that some intersections have unmarked crosswalks. Essentially, the imaginary extension of a sidewalk across a road at an intersection, even if no paint marks it. If you were crossing at an intersection and no marked crosswalk existed, you may have had more legal protection than you realize. These details matter, and they're worth investigating.

Texas Comparative Fault: What the 51% Rule Means for You

Texas uses a system called modified comparative fault, sometimes called proportionate responsibility. Here's the plain version: if you were partially at fault for the accident, your financial recovery gets reduced by your percentage of fault. But if you're found more than 50% at fault, meaning you bear the majority of the blame, you recover nothing under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 33.001.

That 51% threshold is the critical number in pedestrian cases where a crosswalk wasn't involved.

Say a jury determines you were 30% at fault for crossing mid-block without yielding, and the driver was 70% at fault for speeding and failing to watch for pedestrians. If your total damages are $200,000, you'd recover $140,000, reduced by your 30% share of fault. You still recover something real.

Now flip it. If the jury finds you were 60% at fault, because you crossed against traffic unexpectedly at night without looking, you recover zero. That's the insurance company's goal when they push the "no crosswalk" narrative hard.

How Insurers Use the Crosswalk Argument Against You

Insurance adjusters are trained negotiators. They know Texas's 51% rule. When they find out you weren't in a marked crosswalk, they often move fast.

First, they'll contact you quickly, sometimes within hours of the crash, hoping to get a recorded statement before you've spoken with anyone. They'll ask you to walk through exactly what happened, where you were, and where you were going. Every answer you give about your position on the road gets used to build a fault argument.

Second, they'll assign you a high fault percentage in their internal evaluation. Some adjusters will tell you directly: "We're finding you 60% at fault, so we can only offer you 40% of your damages." That number didn't come from a jury or a judge. They made it up, strategically.

Third, they'll make a lowball settlement offer and frame it as generous given "your share" of responsibility. Many injured pedestrians accept it because they don't know they have options.

The adjuster's fault percentage isn't a legal finding. It's a negotiating position. You don't have to accept it.

If an insurance company calls you after a pedestrian accident, especially one outside a crosswalk, be careful about giving details before you've talked with a personal injury attorney. What you say early in the process shapes everything that comes after.

Factors That Actually Drive Fault in These Cases

When a pedestrian case goes to litigation in Harris County, whether that's a negotiation with an insurance company, mediation, or a jury trial in the 11th District Court, the fault analysis is more nuanced than "crosswalk or no crosswalk." Here are the real factors that move the needle.

Driver speed and attention

A driver traveling 50 mph in a 35 mph zone has reduced reaction time significantly. Even if a pedestrian darted into the road, a speeding driver may have had no reasonable chance to stop. The speeding is still their fault. Crash reconstruction experts can often calculate whether a driver going the speed limit would have had time to avoid the collision. That analysis is powerful evidence.

Visibility conditions

Many of Houston's worst pedestrian crashes happen at dusk, at night, or in rain. Texas law expects drivers to adjust their speed and attention for low-visibility conditions. A driver who hits someone they "couldn't see" because it was dark still has a duty to drive at a speed where they can stop for hazards in their headlight range.

Road design and signage

On stretches of Highway 6 or Westheimer where the nearest marked crosswalk is a quarter mile away and no pedestrian signal exists, a person crossing mid-block isn't being reckless. They're doing what the road's design left them to do. Poor infrastructure can shift fault toward the roadway authority, which opens a different legal avenue entirely.

Driver impairment or distraction

If the driver was intoxicated or on their phone at the time of impact, that evidence dramatically affects the comparative fault analysis, regardless of where the pedestrian was crossing. A distracted driver on I-45 bears substantial responsibility for the consequences of their inattention.

Your actions at the time

Yes, your behavior matters too. Were you looking before you crossed? Were you wearing dark clothing at night on a road with no lighting? Were you crossing somewhere no pedestrian could reasonably expect to cross safely? These facts hurt your case. An experienced attorney won't pretend otherwise. But they'll also put those facts in context against everything the driver did wrong.

What Evidence Makes or Breaks These Claims

Pedestrian accident cases live and die on evidence. In Houston, much of that evidence disappears fast. Traffic cameras on city corridors overwrite footage on cycles as short as 24 to 72 hours. Skid marks fade. Witnesses move on.

The evidence that most often determines fault includes:

  • Traffic and surveillance camera footage. businesses along Westheimer, Houston metro intersections, and TxDOT cameras on state highways may have captured the impact
  • The driver's phone records. subpoenaed in litigation to check for calls or texts at the moment of impact
  • Event data recorder ("black box") data. most vehicles since 2012 record speed, braking, and steering inputs in the seconds before a crash
  • Eyewitness accounts. bystanders, nearby business employees, other drivers
  • Police crash report (CR-3). filed by Houston Police Department or Harris County Sheriff's Office; includes the investigating officer's observations and sometimes a preliminary fault notation
  • Medical records. documenting the nature and severity of your injuries from day one, ideally from a Houston trauma center like Memorial Hermann or Ben Taub

The sooner this evidence is secured, the better. That's one practical reason to talk with an attorney early. Not to file a lawsuit immediately, but to get the right pieces preserved before they're gone.

The Statute of Limitations in Texas Pedestrian Cases

Texas gives most personal injury plaintiffs two years from the date of the accident to file a lawsuit, under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't, especially when you factor in how long it takes to finish medical treatment, understand the full extent of your injuries, and investigate the accident properly.

There are situations where the clock runs differently. If the driver who hit you was a government employee operating a government vehicle, such as a city bus, a TxDOT truck, or a METRO vehicle, different notice deadlines apply, and they're much shorter. Missing those deadlines can end your case before it begins.

If a minor was injured, the limitations period typically doesn't start running until they turn 18. That's a nuance worth confirming with an attorney for your specific facts.

What a Houston Pedestrian Accident Claim Actually Covers

If you have a valid claim, even one where your fault percentage reduces your recovery, Texas law allows you to seek damages for:

  • Past and future medical expenses (emergency care, surgery, physical therapy, ongoing treatment)
  • Lost wages and lost earning capacity if your injuries affect your ability to work
  • Physical pain and suffering, past and future
  • Mental anguish
  • Disfigurement, if the crash left permanent scarring or physical changes
  • Physical impairment, separate from pain and suffering under Texas law

In cases involving a drunk driver or a driver who acted with gross recklessness, Texas law also permits exemplary damages, a penalty on top of actual damages meant to punish extreme misconduct. These aren't available in every case, but they're worth evaluating when the driver's conduct was especially egregious.

If someone was killed in a pedestrian accident, surviving family members may have a wrongful death claim under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.002. The same comparative fault principles apply, but the damages calculation and who has the right to bring the claim follow different rules.

Practical Steps if You Were Hit Outside a Crosswalk

You can't go back and change where you were standing when the car hit you. But you can control what happens next. Here's what matters most in the hours and days following the crash.

Get medical attention immediately. Even if you think you're okay. Adrenaline masks pain. Traumatic brain injuries, internal injuries, and soft-tissue damage don't always announce themselves right away. Going to a Houston emergency room or urgent care clinic that day creates a medical record tied to the date of the accident. That documentation matters enormously later.

Don't give a recorded statement to the insurance company. The adjuster may tell you it's routine or required. It isn't. You have no legal obligation to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer. Politely decline and say you'll be in touch through your attorney.

Document what you can from the scene. If you're physically able, photograph the road, your position, the vehicle, any skid marks, traffic signals, and nearby signage. Take photos of your injuries. Get names and contact information from any witnesses.

Write down what happened while it's fresh. Memory degrades fast. A detailed account of the sequence of events, what you saw, heard, and did before the impact, is far more useful when written on day one than recalled six months later.

Talk to an attorney before accepting anything. A consultation costs you nothing and tells you where your case actually stands. Before you sign a release or accept a check from any insurer, understand what you're giving up. Settlements are final. You can't come back later if your injuries turn out to be more serious than they first appeared.
