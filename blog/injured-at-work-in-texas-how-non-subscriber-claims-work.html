Injured at Work in Texas? What Happens When Your Employer Skipped Workers' Comp

Texas is the only state where private employers can opt out of workers' compensation entirely. Hundreds of thousands of workers don't know their employer did exactly that. Here's what it means for you if you're hurt on the job.

You got hurt at work. Maybe a forklift clipped you in a warehouse off Beltway 8. Maybe you fell from scaffolding at a construction site in Katy. Maybe a piece of equipment failed and took off part of your hand.

Your first assumption, the same one most people make, is that workers' compensation covers you. You'll file a claim, get your medical bills paid, collect some wage replacement while you heal, and eventually go back to work.

Then you find out your employer is a non-subscriber. They never bought workers' comp insurance. And suddenly the path forward looks completely different from anything you expected.

This is a good moment to stop and actually understand what that means. In Texas, being an injured employee of a non-subscriber is not necessarily worse than being covered by workers' comp. In some ways, it's significantly better.

Texas Is the Only State Where This Is Legal

Every other state in the country requires private employers to carry workers' compensation insurance. Texas does not. Under Texas law, workers' comp coverage is entirely voluntary for most private employers.

That single fact reshapes everything about workplace injury claims in this state.

Estimates put the share of Texas private employers who have opted out (called "non-subscribers") somewhere between 25% and 40%. That's not a fringe phenomenon. Some of the largest employers in the Houston area are non-subscribers, including major retailers and distribution companies whose workers handle physically demanding jobs every day.

If your employer is one of them and you get hurt, you are not without options. You're just on a different legal track than a standard workers' comp claim. And that track has real teeth.

How to Find Out If Your Employer Is a Non-Subscriber

Texas employers are required to notify employees in writing whether they carry workers' comp coverage. That notice should be posted at your workplace and given to you when you're hired. Under Texas Labor Code § 406.005, non-subscribing employers must file an annual notice with the Texas Department of Insurance, Division of Workers' Compensation.

In practice, a lot of workers never read that notice or don't remember seeing it. Here's how to check:

  • Look at your onboarding paperwork. Non-subscribers are required to give new hires written notice of their non-subscriber status.
  • Check the Texas Department of Insurance's online database. You can search by employer name to see whether a company has filed as a non-subscriber.
  • Ask your HR department directly. They're required to tell you.
  • Call an attorney. If you've been injured and you're not sure, a quick consultation can answer that question fast, and it costs you nothing.

What Changes When Your Employer Doesn't Have Workers' Comp

Workers' compensation is a trade-off system. Employees give up the right to sue their employer in exchange for automatic, no-fault coverage: medical bills paid, partial wage replacement, no need to prove anyone was negligent. It's predictable. It's also capped.

When your employer is a non-subscriber, that trade-off never happened. You kept your right to sue.

That matters enormously. In a standard workers' comp claim in Texas, you generally cannot recover for pain and suffering, full lost wages, or loss of future earning capacity. The system pays a formula, not what your injury actually cost you as a person.

In a non-subscriber lawsuit, you can pursue the full range of damages a negligence claim allows: past and future medical expenses, lost income, reduced earning capacity, pain and suffering, and in some cases damages for how the injury has changed your daily life.

The non-subscriber system in Texas was designed to give employers flexibility. But it also removes the legal shields they'd have under workers' comp. That's real negotiating power you can use.

The Big Advantage: Non-Subscribers Can't Use Most Common Defenses

Here's where the law gets notably favorable for injured workers.

Under Texas Labor Code § 406.033, when an employer is a non-subscriber and an employee is injured, the employer cannot use three of the most powerful defenses available in a typical negligence lawsuit:

  • Contributory negligence. the argument that the worker was partly at fault
  • Assumption of risk. the argument that the worker knew the job was dangerous and accepted that risk
  • Fellow servant rule. the argument that a coworker's negligence, not the employer's, caused the injury

Stripping those defenses away is a big deal. In most personal injury cases in Texas, the defense team spends enormous energy trying to shift some of the blame onto you. Modified comparative fault is one of the most common tools insurers use to drive down settlements. Basically, Texas reduces what you can recover by your share of the blame.

Non-subscriber cases largely sidestep that. You still have to show the employer's negligence caused your injury. But the employer can't walk in and say "well, you should have known better" or "a coworker made a mistake, not us."

What You Have to Prove in a Non-Subscriber Case

This is the part people sometimes misunderstand. A non-subscriber lawsuit is not automatic. You do have to prove that your employer's negligence caused your injury. The burden of proof is on you.

Negligence in a workplace context usually means one of a few things:

  • The employer failed to maintain a safe work environment
  • The employer didn't provide adequate safety equipment or training
  • The employer knew about a dangerous condition and did nothing about it
  • A supervisor or manager's actions directly caused the injury

Building that case requires evidence: incident reports, OSHA logs, maintenance records, witness statements, photos of the scene, and your medical records. The earlier you start gathering that evidence, the stronger your position. Evidence disappears fast at job sites. Equipment gets repaired. Witnesses move on.

If you've been injured at a Harris County work site, a Montgomery County warehouse, or anywhere else in the Houston region, the clock on preserving that evidence started the moment you got hurt.

What a 2025 Texas Supreme Court Ruling Changed

In In re East Texas Medical Center Athens, decided in 2025, the Texas Supreme Court clarified important procedural rules that apply to non-subscriber litigation, specifically around how discovery and jurisdictional disputes are handled when these cases are filed in state court.

The practical effect: it's harder for employers and their insurers to use procedural maneuvering to delay or derail legitimate non-subscriber claims in Texas state courts. The ruling reinforced that injured workers have a clear path to pursue these cases without getting bogged down in threshold procedural fights designed to exhaust them financially before the case ever gets to the merits.

That's a genuinely positive development for non-subscriber plaintiffs. It's also a signal that non-subscriber litigation is active, contested, and worth defending, which tells you something about the real stakes these cases can carry.

What Employers Do Instead of Carrying Workers' Comp

Non-subscriber employers don't just leave themselves exposed. Most of them buy something called an "occupational accident" policy, a private insurance product that looks like workers' comp from the outside but operates very differently.

These policies are not regulated the same way workers' comp is. They're written to protect the employer's interests, not yours. They commonly include:

  • Benefit caps well below what a lawsuit might recover
  • Short windows to report your injury and file a claim
  • Requirements that you sign documents limiting your rights before receiving any benefits
  • Clauses that deny coverage if you don't follow specific internal procedures

This is where a lot of injured workers get hurt a second time. They accept a small payout from an occupational accident policy, sign a release, and later realize they gave up the right to sue for a fraction of what their case was worth.

Do not sign anything from your employer or their insurance company after a serious workplace injury without talking to an attorney first. That's not alarmism. That's just the reality of how these policies are structured.

An occupational accident policy is not workers' comp. It was written by the employer's insurer, for the employer's benefit. Read it, and read it with help, before you accept anything.

The Deadline You Cannot Miss

Texas law gives injured workers two years to file a personal injury lawsuit. That deadline comes from Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't, once you account for everything a non-subscriber case requires: gathering records, identifying witnesses, retaining experts, building the liability picture.

Waiting also hurts your case in a practical way. Security camera footage gets overwritten. Coworkers who saw what happened move to other jobs or other cities. Equipment gets replaced or repaired. The sooner you move, the more of that evidence is still available.

Some non-subscriber policies have their own internal deadlines, sometimes as short as 30 or 90 days to report your injury. Missing those deadlines can complicate your claim even if you still have time to file a lawsuit. Get legal advice early.

Steps to Take Right Now If You Were Hurt at Work

The first week after a workplace injury is when you make decisions that will shape everything that follows. Here's what matters most:

  1. Get medical care immediately. Don't wait. If you're at a job site in the Houston area, Memorial Hermann, HCA Houston Healthcare, and Harris Health System all have trauma-capable facilities. Your health comes first, and your medical records become the backbone of your claim.
  2. Report the injury to your employer in writing. Verbal reports get forgotten or disputed. Put it in writing, keep a copy, note the date and who you told.
  3. Document everything you can. Photos of the scene, photos of your injuries, names and contact information of coworkers who witnessed what happened. If you can write down a detailed account of exactly what occurred while it's fresh, do it.
  4. Find out whether your employer carries workers' comp. The answer to that question changes your entire legal strategy.
  5. Don't sign anything from your employer or their insurer. Not until you've spoken with an attorney about what it says and what you'd be giving up.
  6. Talk to an attorney before you give a recorded statement. If an adjuster calls, and they will call fast, you are not required to give a recorded statement on the spot. What you say can be used to limit your recovery later.

What a Non-Subscriber Case Actually Looks Like

Most non-subscriber cases in Texas don't go to trial. They resolve through negotiation or mediation. But the credible threat of a jury trial, in Harris County District Court or wherever the case is venued, is what drives fair settlements.

Non-subscriber cases that go to trial are decided by juries who hear the full human story: what your injury did to your life, your family, your ability to work and do the things you used to do. That's very different from a workers' comp administrative proceeding, which is a bureaucratic process decided by hearing officers applying statutory formulas.

Juries in Texas can and do hold employers accountable when the evidence shows they put workers in danger and refused to take reasonable precautions. That accountability, the real possibility of it, is exactly why non-subscriber cases carry serious negotiating weight.

If you've been seriously hurt and your employer is a non-subscriber, you are not in a hopeless situation. You may actually be in a stronger position than an employee covered by workers' comp.
