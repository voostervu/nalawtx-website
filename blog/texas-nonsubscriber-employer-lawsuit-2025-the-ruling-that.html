The 2025 Texas Supreme Court Ruling That Shifted the Ground Under Workplace Injury Lawsuits

If your employer opted out of workers' comp and you got hurt on the job, a landmark April 2025 ruling means the company can now point fingers at other parties and potentially reduce what it owes you. Here's exactly what changed and what it means for your case.

Texas is the only state in the country that lets employers skip workers' compensation insurance entirely. Employers that do this are called nonsubscribers. When you work for one and get injured on the job, you can't file a workers' comp claim. But you can sue the employer directly in civil court. Historically, that lawsuit came with a major advantage for injured workers: the employer couldn't use your own negligence against you the same way it could in an ordinary injury case.

On April 25, 2025, the Texas Supreme Court issued its opinion in In re East Texas Medical Center Athens (No. 23-1039). The ruling changed one critical piece of how those nonsubscriber lawsuits work, and not in workers' favor. If you've been hurt on the job in Texas and your employer opted out of workers' comp, you need to understand what this decision means before you talk to an adjuster, sign anything, or assume your case looks the same as it would have a year ago.

First: What Is a Nonsubscriber Employer?

Under Texas law, private employers can choose not to carry workers' compensation insurance. There's no penalty for opting out. It's completely legal. Roughly one-third of Texas employers are nonsubscribers, and that number is especially high in industries like construction, retail, and food service.

When an employer subscribes to workers' comp, an injured employee gets a no-fault benefit system: medical bills and some lost wages, in exchange for giving up the right to sue the employer. When an employer doesn't subscribe, the injured worker keeps the right to sue. That lawsuit is governed by Texas Labor Code § 406.033, which strips the nonsubscribing employer of several defenses it would normally have, including the ability to blame the employee's own negligence or the negligence of a coworker.

That's been the core of what makes nonsubscriber lawsuits powerful for injured workers. Your employer can't say "it was your own fault" and use that to gut your case. Or at least, that was the rule before April 2025.

What the East Texas Medical Center Athens Ruling Actually Decided

The Texas Supreme Court's decision in In re East Texas Medical Center Athens addressed whether a nonsubscribing employer can designate a responsible third party under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code Chapter 33.

Here's what that means in plain English. Chapter 33 is Texas's proportionate liability law. It governs how fault gets divided up when more than one party contributed to an injury. Under Chapter 33, a defendant can point to someone else (a "responsible third party") and ask the jury to assign some of the fault to that person or company instead. That third party doesn't even have to be in the courtroom. If the jury assigns them 40% of the fault, the defendant's liability can shrink by that same 40%.

Before this ruling, nonsubscribers had a problem using Chapter 33. The Labor Code already stripped them of certain defenses, and courts were divided on whether designating a responsible third party was just another back-door way to sneak in those forbidden defenses. The Texas Supreme Court resolved that split, ruling that yes, nonsubscribing employers can designate responsible third parties under Chapter 33.

The practical effect: an employer that would have been on the hook for 100% of your damages can now tell the jury that a contractor, an equipment manufacturer, or another company shares the blame, and walk away paying less.

What the Employer Can. and Still Cannot. Do

It's worth being precise here, because this ruling is being misread in both directions.

What changed: Nonsubscribing employers can now designate responsible third parties under Chapter 33 and argue to the jury that someone else (a subcontractor, an equipment maker, a staffing agency, a premises owner) bears part of the fault. If the jury agrees, your employer's share of liability goes down.

What did not change: The Labor Code's core protections still stand. A nonsubscribing employer still cannot use your own negligence as a defense. It cannot blame a coworker's negligence to escape liability. Those defenses, removed by Texas Labor Code § 406.033, remain off the table.

So the employer can't say "you were being careless." But it can now say "that third-party equipment rental company was also responsible." The distinction matters, and it's one a defense lawyer will exploit if they can.

Why This Matters for Workers in Harris County and Beyond

Think about how construction sites in Houston actually work. On a typical job site in Harris County, say a commercial build in the Energy Corridor or a residential project near Katy, you might have a general contractor, multiple subcontractors, a staffing agency providing labor, and equipment owned by a rental company. Your direct employer might be a subcontractor with relatively thin assets.

Before this ruling, if that employer was a nonsubscriber and you got hurt because of dangerous site conditions they controlled, they couldn't point at the general contractor or the equipment company to dilute their fault. Now they can try.

For workers, that creates two new problems. First, your employer now has a litigation tool to reduce what it owes you, even if it was clearly responsible. Second, you and your attorney need to think more broadly from the start, identifying every potentially responsible party early, because the employer is now motivated to point at all of them.

This also changes settlement dynamics. Before the ruling, a nonsubscriber facing a clear liability case had fewer places to hide. Now defense counsel has a new argument to bring to mediation. That doesn't mean your case is weaker on the merits. It means the defense playbook got longer.

The Silver Lining: Multiple Defendants Can Mean More Recovery

Here's the flip side that's easy to miss in the initial reaction to this ruling. If your employer designates a third party as responsible and the evidence actually supports it, that third party is now a viable defendant in your case too.

Say your employer points at an equipment manufacturer whose machine malfunctioned and caused your injury. Your attorney can now pursue both the employer and the manufacturer. If the jury assigns 60% fault to the employer and 40% to the manufacturer, you may be able to collect from both.

The key is acting fast. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code Chapter 33, a responsible third party designation triggers deadlines for the plaintiff to add them as a defendant. Miss that window and you might be limited in what you can recover from them. This is another reason why getting an attorney involved quickly after a workplace injury is so important, not just for preserving evidence, but for protecting your legal options as the litigation strategy develops.

The ruling cuts both ways. Your employer can use it to reduce its share of fault. You can use it to bring more defendants into the case and potentially increase the total pool of recovery.

How Employers Will Use This. and How to Prepare for It

Defense attorneys for nonsubscribing employers started working this ruling into their strategy almost immediately after the April 2025 decision. Here's what that looks like in practice.

Early investigation into third parties. Employers and their insurers will start investigating potential third-party targets from day one: equipment manufacturers, property owners, general contractors, staffing agencies. They're looking for anyone they can plausibly point to.

Aggressive third-party designations. Expect defense lawyers to designate responsible third parties broadly, sometimes as a tactical move to create confusion at trial even when the third party's fault is marginal. The goal is to get the jury thinking about shared responsibility.

Pressure in settlement talks. A third-party designation gives the employer's insurer a talking point to justify a lower offer. They'll argue their client's proportionate share is reduced, so the settlement number should be too.

The counter to all of this is preparation. Document the scene of your accident before anything is cleaned up or repaired. Identify and preserve evidence related to any equipment, machinery, or conditions that contributed to your injury. Get witness information. And talk to a personal injury attorney who handles workplace injury cases before the employer's legal team has had weeks to build their narrative.

The Statute of Limitations Still Applies. Don't Delay

Nothing about this ruling changed the deadline to file your lawsuit. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, you generally have two years from the date of your injury to file a personal injury claim in Texas. That clock starts the day you're hurt, not the day you understand what happened, not the day you finish treatment.

Two years sounds like a lot. It goes fast. Medical treatment takes time. Evidence disappears. Witnesses move or forget details. And now, with third-party designation strategy in play from the earliest days of a case, you have less runway to wait before engaging an attorney.

If you were injured on a job site anywhere in the greater Houston area (Harris County, Fort Bend County, Montgomery County, Galveston County) and your employer opted out of workers' comp, the clock is running.

Questions to Ask When You Talk to an Attorney

If you consult with a lawyer about a nonsubscriber workplace injury claim, here's what's worth discussing specifically in light of this ruling:

  • Has the employer already designated any responsible third parties? If litigation has started or a claim has been filed, find out immediately.
  • What third parties might legitimately share responsibility? A good attorney will investigate this independently, not just react to who the employer nominates.
  • What's the deadline to add those third parties as defendants? Chapter 33 has procedural requirements, and missing them has consequences.
  • How does this affect the valuation of my case? The answer depends on the facts, the identity of the third parties, and their assets or insurance coverage.

The Bigger Picture: Texas Still Protects Injured Workers, But You Have to Fight for It

Texas has always been an unusual state when it comes to workplace injury law. Opting out of workers' comp is legal here. The Legislature made a deliberate choice when it wrote those nonsubscriber protections into the Labor Code: if an employer was going to skip the no-fault system, it had to give up some defenses in exchange.

The East Texas Medical Center Athens decision doesn't eliminate those protections. Your employer still can't blame you. A coworker's negligence still can't be used against you. Those rights are intact.

What changed is that employers now have a new tool to distribute fault, and well-resourced employers with experienced defense counsel will use it. That's not a reason to give up on your claim. It's a reason to take it seriously from the start, move quickly, and work with an attorney who understands how things actually work after this ruling.

Texas workers deserve to know their rights. This ruling complicates the picture. It doesn't erase it.
