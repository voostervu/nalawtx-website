Texas Distracted Driving Laws and Your Injury Claim: What Houston Victims Need to Know About Negligence Per Se

Nearly one in six Texas crashes in 2023 involved a distracted driver. If that driver broke Texas's cell phone law, it may already be doing much of the legal heavy lifting in your injury case.

Picture the intersection of I-10 and Beltway 8 on a Tuesday afternoon. Traffic slows, then stops. A driver three cars back doesn't notice because they're reading a text. The crash happens in under two seconds. You're the one with whiplash, a totaled car, and a stack of medical bills.

Now the other driver's insurance company is calling. They're friendly. They're asking questions. And they haven't mentioned, even once, that their driver may have violated Texas law at the moment of impact.

That law matters to your claim. Here's why, and what you should do about it.

Texas's Cell Phone Law: What It Actually Says

Texas passed a statewide hands-free law in 2017. It's codified at Texas Transportation Code § 545.4251. The core rule is straightforward: drivers cannot read, write, or send electronic messages, including texts, emails, and social media posts, while operating a motor vehicle on a public road.

The law applies everywhere in Texas. It doesn't matter whether you're on I-45 in Harris County or a two-lane county road outside of Beaumont. If you're behind the wheel and the car is moving, putting your eyes on your phone screen to compose or read a message is a criminal violation.

First offense: up to a $99 fine. If the crash caused serious injury or death: up to $4,000 and a misdemeanor charge. Those criminal penalties are separate from what happens in your civil injury claim. But they connect directly to it.

What "Negligence Per Se" Means (In Plain English)

In a typical car accident case, your attorney has to prove four things: the other driver owed you a duty of care, they breached it, that breach caused your injury, and you suffered real damages. The first two elements, duty and breach, usually take work. You have to show the driver was careless.

Negligence per se cuts that work in half.

When a driver breaks a safety statute, a law designed to protect people from a specific type of harm, Texas courts can treat that violation as automatic proof of duty and breach. You don't have to argue that texting while driving was careless. The Texas Legislature already decided it was careless when it passed the law.

Translated: if the other driver was texting and Texas Transportation Code § 545.4251 applies, they were negligent as a matter of law. Your job shifts to proving causation and damages, not whether they were behaving reasonably.

That's a real advantage. It simplifies arguments at trial, makes early settlement negotiations more direct, and gives your attorney a concrete legal hook to point to from day one.

Does the Law Cover Every Type of Distraction?

No. This is an important limit to understand. Texas Transportation Code § 545.4251 specifically targets electronic messaging. Scrolling through a playlist, adjusting a navigation app, or watching a video could potentially fall under this statute depending on the facts, but the plain language focuses on reading and sending messages.

Other distracted driving behavior, eating, applying makeup, turning around to talk to a passenger, rubbernecking at a wreck on the Southwest Freeway, isn't covered by the same statute. That doesn't make those behaviors legal or defensible. It just means your claim would proceed under traditional negligence rather than negligence per se.

The distinction affects strategy, not necessarily outcome. A driver who causes a crash while eating a burger can still be fully liable. The path to proving it is just a little longer.

How Phone Records Become Evidence

Proving a driver was on their phone at the moment of impact is one of the most important, and most time-sensitive, tasks in a distracted driving case.

Cell phone carriers keep call and data records. Text timestamps are logged. App usage can be tracked. But carriers don't hold onto detailed records indefinitely, and accessing them requires a proper legal request, typically a subpoena served during litigation or a formal preservation demand sent before a lawsuit is filed.

Here's the practical problem: by the time most injury victims call an attorney, weeks have passed. Records that might have shown the driver was actively texting at 2:47 p.m. on the day of the crash are already at risk of being overwritten or purged.

Getting an attorney involved early, ideally within days of the crash, gives your legal team time to send preservation letters and initiate discovery before critical evidence disappears.

Other evidence worth preserving: the police report (officers sometimes note that a driver admitted to phone use), witness statements, dashcam footage from nearby vehicles or business security cameras, and any social media posts the driver made around the time of the crash. Houston's dense urban grid, especially around areas like the Galleria, Downtown, and the Medical Center, means there are often cameras nearby that captured the moments before impact.

What the Insurance Company Does With This Information

Insurance adjusters know about negligence per se. They're counting on you not to.

When they call you in the first 24 to 48 hours, and they will call, they're trying to get a recorded statement before you've spoken to an attorney. They'll ask open-ended questions about the accident. They may ask whether you "noticed" the other driver doing anything before impact. They're listening for anything they can use to reduce your claim.

If you've already said on record that you "didn't see" the other driver's phone, that makes it harder later when phone records surface showing they were mid-text. Your own statement gets used against the weight of the evidence.

The safest approach: be polite, get the adjuster's name and contact information, and tell them you'll be in touch after speaking with an attorney. You are not legally required to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company. You're not required to guess, speculate, or fill silence with details you're unsure about.

For a closer look at how these calls work and exactly what to say, read our post on how to handle insurance adjuster calls after a Houston crash.

Texas's Modified Comparative Fault Rule and Why It Matters Here

Texas uses a system called modified comparative fault. Basically, the state reduces what you can recover by your percentage of blame for the accident. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 33.001, if you're found to be more than 50% at fault, you recover nothing.

Insurance companies know this. In distracted driving cases, they often try to assign some fault to the victim. You were going slightly over the speed limit, you failed to brake in time, you had a light that "could have been" yellow. Each percentage point of fault they pin on you is a percentage point less they have to pay.

Negligence per se works as a counterweight here. When the other driver's violation of § 545.4251 is established, it's harder for the defense to pivot the conversation toward your behavior. The legal record already shows what they did wrong. That makes comparative fault arguments more difficult to land.

It's not a guarantee, nothing in litigation is, but it shifts the center of gravity in your favor.

Steps to Take Right Now If a Distracted Driver Hit You

The decisions you make in the first few days after a crash shape your entire claim. Here's what actually helps:

  • Get medical attention immediately. Even if you feel fine, adrenaline masks injury. Soft tissue damage, concussions, and internal injuries can take days to surface. Houston has major trauma centers, Memorial Hermann, Ben Taub, Houston Methodist, with emergency care equipped to document crash-related injuries. Your medical records are evidence.
  • File a police report if you haven't already. In Harris County, you can file an online crash report with the Texas Department of Transportation for crashes where law enforcement didn't respond. The report creates an official record of the incident.
  • Document everything you saw. Write down what you remember: the exact time, the road, the conditions, the other driver's behavior before impact. Did you see them looking down? Did they brake at all? Write it down now, while it's fresh. Don't post it publicly.
  • Photograph the scene, the vehicles, and your injuries. Skid marks, vehicle positions, and visible damage tell a story. So do bruises, lacerations, and swelling. Photograph them over the first several days as they develop.
  • Don't give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurer. See above. Worth repeating.
  • Contact a Texas personal injury attorney early. The preservation window for phone records and surveillance footage is short. Early involvement by an attorney can make the difference between having that evidence and not having it.

How Distracted Driving Claims Actually Resolve

Most Texas personal injury claims, including distracted driving cases, settle before trial. When the evidence of a statutory violation is strong, the insurance company has less room to argue liability. Negotiations tend to focus more on the value of your damages: medical bills, lost wages, pain and suffering, future care costs.

Cases go to trial when liability is genuinely disputed, when the insurer low-balls and won't move, or when the damages are severe enough that the settlement offers don't reflect reality. Harris County District Courts, in the courthouse complex Downtown on Congress Avenue, handle civil personal injury trials. Juries here see distracted driving cases regularly.

What drives value in these claims isn't just the statutory violation. It's the documentation of your injury, the consistency of your medical treatment, and the paper trail showing the full impact on your life. That's why the steps above matter so much. A negligence per se hook is valuable. It's even more valuable when it's paired with solid evidence of what your injury actually cost you.

A Word on Truck Drivers and Commercial Vehicles

If a commercial driver, a trucker, a rideshare driver, a delivery vehicle operator, was the distracted party, the analysis gets more complex. Federal motor carrier regulations layer on top of Texas state law. Commercial drivers are held to stricter standards, and the companies employing them may share liability for the crash.

Harris County sees significant commercial truck traffic along the Port of Houston corridors, on I-610, and along US-59/I-69. If your crash involved a commercial vehicle, the evidence preservation issue is even more urgent. Electronic logging devices and in-cab cameras have data retention windows that close fast. The Statute of Limitations: Don't Wait Too Long

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, you generally have two years from the date of a crash to file a personal injury lawsuit in Texas. Miss that deadline and the court will almost certainly dismiss your case, regardless of how strong your negligence per se argument is.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't, once you factor in medical treatment, insurance negotiations, attorney investigation, and the time it takes to build a complete damages picture. Cases filed under time pressure, rushed to the deadline, tend to be weaker than cases built with room to breathe.

Contacting an attorney sooner gives your team time to do the work properly.
