Tesla Autopilot Killed Someone in Texas. Who Pays?

A Harris County wrongful death lawsuit filed in June 2026 is putting a sharp legal lens on Tesla Autopilot crashes in Texas. It's answering a question more families here may soon have to ask.

By Vu Nguyen, Founder & Managing Partner · Updated November 25, 2026

When Tesla Autopilot is involved in a fatal Texas crash, the family may have claims against both Tesla (for a defective product) and the driver (for negligence). Texas law allows both theories in one lawsuit, with a jury assigning fault percentages to each party. The deadline to file is two years from the date of death under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003.

  • Texas product liability law covers three defect types: design, manufacturing, and marketing — all potentially apply to Autopilot crashes.
  • Surviving spouses, children, and parents can sue for wrongful death under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004.
  • Tesla's vehicle data logs Autopilot engagement and driver inputs — that evidence must be preserved quickly after a crash.
  • Texas's two-year filing deadline sounds long, but pre-litigation investigation for a product case can take six to twelve months.

A 76-year-old woman was inside her Katy home when a Tesla Model 3 using Autopilot veered off the road and crashed into it. She didn't survive. On June 24, 2026, her family filed a wrongful death lawsuit in Harris County District Court.

The case is getting national attention, partly because it echoes a $243 million Florida verdict against Tesla involving a similar Autopilot-related death. But for Texans, this Harris County filing matters for a different reason. It's happening in our backyard, under Texas law, and it's asking courts here to decide something that affects anyone who shares the road, or lives near one, with a vehicle running on driver-assist technology.

This post breaks down what that lawsuit likely argues, how Texas law handles product liability and wrongful death claims, and what any family facing a similar tragedy needs to understand about their options.

What Autopilot Actually Does, and What Tesla Says It Doesn't Do

Tesla's Autopilot is a Level 2 driver-assistance system. That classification, Level 2, is key to understanding the liability fight. Under the SAE's framework, Level 2 means the system controls steering and speed, but the human driver must remain fully attentive and ready to take over at any moment.

Tesla has consistently said that Autopilot is not a self-driving feature. The name, critics argue, says otherwise. So does how drivers use it. National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigations into prior Tesla crashes have found a pattern: drivers misunderstood what Autopilot could and couldn't do, over-trusted the system, and failed to intervene in time.

That gap between what the technology actually does and what drivers reasonably believe it does is the heart of product liability litigation against Tesla. That's true in Florida, in California, and now in Harris County, Texas.

The Two Legal Theories the Katy Lawsuit Almost Certainly Relies On

Wrongful death lawsuits involving driver-assist technology don't fit cleanly into the standard car-accident claim framework. When a human driver runs a red light, negligence is straightforward. When an Autopilot system fails to recognize a residential structure and steers into it, the legal picture involves at least two separate defendants and two distinct theories of liability.

1. Product Liability Against Tesla

Under Texas law, a product manufacturer can be held liable when a product is defective. Texas recognizes three types of product defects: a design defect (the product is inherently dangerous as designed), a manufacturing defect (something went wrong during production), and a marketing defect (the warnings or instructions failed to adequately inform users of risks).

In a Tesla Autopilot case, all three are potentially in play. A design defect claim says the Autopilot system itself was unreasonably dangerous, that it couldn't reliably distinguish between a road and a structure, or that it allowed hands-off driving in conditions where it shouldn't. A marketing defect claim, which is a powerful theory here, says Tesla's branding and instructions led drivers to believe the system was more capable than it was, without adequate warnings about its limits.

Texas product liability claims are grounded in Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 82.001 et seq. Texas also allows strict liability for product defects, meaning the plaintiff doesn't have to prove Tesla was careless. Only that the product was defective and that defect caused the harm.

2. Negligence Against the Driver

Even when Autopilot is engaged, the driver behind the wheel has a legal duty to maintain control of the vehicle. Texas transportation law requires drivers to operate vehicles safely regardless of what technology they're using. If the driver in the Katy crash took their eyes off the road, failed to respond to warning signals, or was otherwise inattentive, the family can pursue a negligence claim against the driver as well.

These two theories aren't mutually exclusive. The lawsuit can argue both: that the product was defective and that the driver was negligent. Texas's modified comparative fault rules then govern how fault gets allocated among all responsible parties.

In Texas, you can name multiple defendants in a single lawsuit. The jury assigns each party a percentage of fault. As long as the defendant you're collecting from is at least 51% responsible, you can recover from them. That structure matters enormously in an Autopilot case with both a corporate defendant and an individual driver.

Why the Florida $243 Million Verdict Matters, But Isn't Binding in Texas

In 2023, a Florida jury awarded $243 million to the family of an Apple engineer who died when his Tesla on Autopilot hit a truck. The jury found Tesla liable for failing to warn drivers about Autopilot's limitations. It was one of the largest product liability verdicts in U.S. history involving a consumer vehicle technology.

That verdict doesn't legally control what happens in Harris County. Texas courts follow Texas law, and the Katy case will be decided by a Texas jury under Texas standards. But the Florida case matters for two reasons: it showed that juries are willing to hold Tesla accountable for Autopilot failures, and it generated extensive discovery. Internal Tesla documents, engineering reports, communications about known Autopilot deficiencies. That material may now be available to plaintiffs in other cases.

Prior litigation can expose a company's internal knowledge of a defect. If Tesla's own engineers documented concerns about Autopilot's ability to detect stationary or off-road objects and those concerns were overridden, that evidence can be devastating in a Texas courtroom.

Wrongful Death Claims in Texas: Who Can Sue and What They Can Recover

Texas has a specific statute governing who can bring a wrongful death lawsuit. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004, the right to sue belongs to the surviving spouse, children, and parents of the person killed. If those individuals don't file within three months, the executor or administrator of the estate can file on behalf of the estate.

What can a Texas wrongful death claim recover? The damages fall into two categories.

The first covers losses specific to the surviving family members: lost financial support the deceased would have provided, loss of companionship and society (the relationship itself, not just the economic contribution), mental anguish, and loss of services. These are personal to whoever is suing.

The second is a survival claim, a separate legal action brought on behalf of the deceased person's estate. It covers what the deceased suffered before death: medical expenses, physical pain and suffering, and mental anguish experienced between the crash and the moment of death.

In a case like the Katy crash, where the victim was at home and not even in the car, both types of claims are likely in play. For more detail on how Texas wrongful death cases work, this post covers the full framework.

The Deadline That Can't Be Missed

Texas's statute of limitations for personal injury and wrongful death is two years from the date of injury or death. That deadline is set by Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Miss it, and no matter how strong the case is, the courthouse door closes.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't. Product liability cases against a company like Tesla require serious pre-litigation investigation: gathering the vehicle's onboard data (Tesla's vehicles continuously log Autopilot engagement, speed, steering inputs, and driver monitoring data), retaining engineering experts, reviewing any prior incidents involving the same model or software version, and building the factual record before a lawsuit is ever filed.

That work takes time. Often six to twelve months just to get to a complaint worth filing. Families who wait until month twenty-two to call a lawyer are starting from a hole.

The Katy family filed six months after the crash. That's the right move. It preserves evidence, gives attorneys time to build the case before Tesla's legal team has a chance to shape the narrative, and keeps all options open.

Tesla's Likely Defense Arguments, and How Texas Law Answers Them

Tesla has used several recurring defense strategies in Autopilot litigation. Understanding them matters if you're evaluating a similar case.

Driver assumption of risk. Tesla's Autopilot terms require drivers to acknowledge the system's limitations and agree to maintain attention. Tesla often argues that by accepting those terms, drivers assumed the risks of using Autopilot. Under Texas law, assumption of risk is a valid defense. But it's not absolute. A manufacturer still can't disclaim liability for a defect it knew about and failed to adequately warn against. If the warnings were buried in a click-through screen or insufficient given the actual danger, Texas courts can find them inadequate.

Driver negligence as the proximate cause. Tesla frequently argues that the driver's failure to pay attention, not the technology, caused the crash. In Texas, this plays into the comparative fault framework. If a jury decides the driver was 80% at fault and Tesla only 20%, the family might recover only 20% of their damages from Tesla. But if Tesla's design and marketing defects contributed substantially to why the driver was inattentive, because the system was built in a way that lulled drivers into complacency, that argument cuts back against Tesla.

Sophisticated user defense. Tesla may argue that Tesla owners who opt into Autopilot are sophisticated users who should understand the technology's limits. Texas courts have recognized a sophisticated user exception in some product liability contexts. How it applies to consumer vehicles marketed to the general public is genuinely unsettled. That's one reason cases like the Katy lawsuit matter for establishing Texas precedent.

What Texas's New AV Law Adds to the Picture

In 2026, Texas enacted new autonomous vehicle legislation that addressed liability frameworks for fully autonomous vehicles operating without a human driver. We covered that law in detail in this post.

But Autopilot is not a fully autonomous system. The Tesla involved in the Katy crash is classified as a Level 2 vehicle. The driver was legally required to remain in control. That distinction matters. The 2026 AV law's framework for operator-less vehicles likely doesn't apply here. This case falls under the older product liability and negligence framework, not the new AV statute.

That's actually significant for plaintiffs. The new AV statute, which Texas passed partly to encourage autonomous vehicle development, contains provisions limiting certain types of claims. Those limits appear designed for fully autonomous commercial deployments. The Katy case, as a Level 2 product liability claim, sits in a different legal lane.

What Families Dealing with an Autopilot Crash Should Know Right Now

If someone you love was killed or seriously injured in a crash involving Tesla Autopilot or any other driver-assist system in Texas, here's what matters immediately.

The vehicle data exists and must be preserved. Tesla vehicles generate continuous logs of Autopilot engagement, driver monitoring camera data, speed, steering, and braking inputs. That data is stored both onboard and in Tesla's servers. It can be obtained through litigation discovery, but it must be requested quickly. Tesla has retention policies, and data can be overwritten. An attorney can send a preservation demand letter to Tesla and to any other custodian of relevant data before it disappears.

Don't talk to Tesla's insurance carrier without counsel. If Tesla's insurer or a claims adjuster calls you, they are not on your side. Their job is to minimize what Tesla pays. They may ask for recorded statements or offer an early settlement that sounds significant but is far less than a fully litigated claim might support. You are not obligated to speak with them. Read this first if an adjuster has already called.

The driver's insurance is only part of the picture. In a standard crash, you pursue the at-fault driver's auto policy. In an Autopilot case, the driver's policy is a floor, not a ceiling. The far larger potential recovery is against Tesla as the product manufacturer. Those are fundamentally different claims requiring different legal strategies, different experts, and different discovery.

Location within Harris County gives you options. The Katy crash family filed in Harris County District Court, the right venue for a case involving a Katy-area incident with Houston-metro defendants. Harris County courts have handled complex product liability litigation and have experienced judges capable of managing this type of case. That familiarity matters.

Why This Case Could Define Texas Autopilot Liability

Right now, there is no Texas appellate court decision squarely addressing what a plaintiff must prove to hold Tesla liable for an Autopilot crash under Texas product liability law. The Katy case may eventually produce one.

If the family's attorneys successfully argue a marketing defect claim, that Tesla's branding, naming, and marketing of Autopilot created a defective and unreasonably dangerous expectation in drivers, that ruling would affect every Autopilot crash case in Texas going forward. It would set the standard for what Tesla must prove to defeat such a claim, and what plaintiffs must show to survive a motion to dismiss or for summary judgment.

That's not hypothetical. The Florida verdict didn't just compensate one family. It established a template. Texas is now writing its own.

Common questions

Can you sue Tesla if Autopilot caused a crash in Texas?

Yes. Under Texas law, a product manufacturer can be held liable when a product has a design, manufacturing, or marketing defect. Texas also allows strict liability for product defects, meaning you don't have to prove Tesla was careless — only that the product was defective and caused the harm. These claims are grounded in Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 82.001 et seq.

Who can file a wrongful death lawsuit in Texas after an Autopilot crash?

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 71.004, the right to sue belongs to the surviving spouse, children, and parents of the person killed. If those individuals don't file within three months, the executor or administrator of the estate can file on behalf of the estate.

What is the deadline to sue Tesla for a wrongful death in Texas?

Texas's statute of limitations for wrongful death is two years from the date of death, set by Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Missing that deadline generally closes the courthouse door regardless of how strong the case is. Because product liability cases require significant pre-litigation investigation, attorneys recommend contacting a lawyer as early as possible.

Does Texas's new autonomous vehicle law protect Tesla from Autopilot lawsuits?

Probably not in a case like the Katy crash. Texas's 2026 autonomous vehicle legislation addressed liability for fully autonomous vehicles operating without a human driver. Autopilot is a Level 2 system, meaning the driver is legally required to remain in control, so the new AV statute's framework for operator-less vehicles likely does not apply. The older product liability and negligence framework governs instead.
