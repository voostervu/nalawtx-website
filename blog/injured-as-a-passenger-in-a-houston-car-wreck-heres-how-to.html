Injured as a Passenger in a Houston Car Wreck? Why You're Almost Never at Fault and How to Claim Against Multiple Drivers

As a passenger, you didn't cause the crash. But getting compensated can still get complicated fast, especially when two drivers are pointing fingers at each other. Here's what Texas law says about your rights and exactly how to protect them.

You were just along for the ride. Maybe a friend was driving you home from a Texans game. Maybe you were in a rideshare crossing the I-10/Loop 610 interchange. Maybe you were a passenger on 290 when another car ran a red light and T-boned you. It doesn't matter how you got there. You were sitting in that seat, you got hurt, and now you're trying to figure out whose insurance pays for it.

Here's the short answer: almost certainly not yours. As a passenger, you typically had no control over what happened. Texas law reflects that. But "not your fault" and "easy to collect" are two different things. When two drivers each blame the other, which happens constantly in Houston two-car collisions, you can find yourself stuck in the middle while your medical bills pile up. This post walks you through how passenger claims actually work in Texas, what to do right now, and what insurance adjusters won't tell you.

Passengers and Fault: The Short Version

Texas uses a fault-based system for car accidents. Whoever caused the crash is financially responsible for injuries. As a passenger, you weren't driving, so in nearly every accident scenario, you bear zero responsibility for what happened.

There are narrow exceptions. If you grabbed the steering wheel, if you were distracting the driver in a way that caused the crash, if you somehow contributed to the collision, a court could find you partially at fault. In practice, those scenarios are rare. The vast majority of passenger injury cases involve fault that belongs entirely to one or both drivers.

Texas follows modified comparative fault. Basically, your ability to recover is reduced by whatever percentage of blame is assigned to you. If a jury found you 10% at fault, you'd recover 10% less. If you're found more than 50% at fault, you recover nothing. For passengers, that threshold almost never comes into play. But it's worth knowing the rule exists under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 33.001.

Who Actually Pays When You're Injured as a Passenger?

This is where passengers get surprised. You have more options than you might think, and potentially more insurance policies available to you than either driver does.

The at-fault driver's liability insurance

Texas requires drivers to carry a minimum of $30,000 per person / $60,000 per accident in bodily injury liability coverage. If one driver caused the crash, their liability policy is your first target. You file a third-party claim directly against the at-fault driver's insurance.

The problem: Texas minimums are low. A single night at Memorial Hermann or Texas Medical Center can exceed $30,000. If the at-fault driver only carries the state minimum, or if they're uninsured, that limit won't cover serious injuries.

The driver of your vehicle

If the driver you were riding with contributed to the crash, their liability insurance is also potentially on the hook for your injuries. This feels awkward when it's your friend or family member. But their insurance is exactly what it's there for, and making a claim against it doesn't necessarily mean suing them personally.

Some passengers hesitate to make a claim against someone they know. That hesitation is understandable, but it usually works against you financially. The driver's insurer, not the driver personally, is the one paying.

Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage (UM/UIM)

If the at-fault driver has no insurance, or not enough to cover your injuries, uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage fills the gap. Under Texas law, insurers are required to offer UM/UIM coverage, though drivers can decline it in writing. If you have your own auto policy, your UM/UIM coverage may apply even though you weren't driving. The driver's policy UM/UIM coverage may also be available to you as a passenger in their vehicle.

Your own health insurance

Your health insurance can cover medical treatment regardless of fault. Using it right away, before any insurance claim settles, keeps you out of medical debt while everything gets sorted out. Your health insurer may later assert a lien against any settlement you receive (this is called subrogation), but that's a problem your attorney helps manage.

The Hardest Scenario: Both Drivers Are Partly at Fault

Two-car accidents in Houston often come down to a dispute. Driver A says Driver B ran the light. Driver B says Driver A was speeding. Both insurers open investigations. Both may deny or minimize liability. Meanwhile, you're the injured passenger with legitimate claims against both of them, and neither insurer is in a hurry to pay.

As a passenger, you can file claims against both drivers simultaneously. You don't have to wait for them to sort out whose fault it was. You don't have to pick one.

Texas law allows you to pursue both drivers, and both insurance policies, at the same time. Each insurer may argue the other driver bears more blame. That's their problem to work out, not yours. Your job is to document your injuries and let your attorney manage the competing claims.

What this looks like practically: you may receive partial offers from each insurer, each pointing at the other driver. Neither offer alone may fully compensate you. An attorney's job in this situation is to negotiate across both policies simultaneously and, if necessary, file suit against both defendants so a Harris County jury can apportion fault between them.

What to Do in the Hours and Days After the Crash

The steps you take right after a crash matter. A lot. Evidence disappears fast in Houston traffic. Here's what to prioritize.

Get medical attention immediately

If you were transported by ambulance, to Ben Taub, Memorial Hermann, Houston Methodist, or anywhere else, follow up with every appointment. If you weren't taken by ambulance, still get evaluated the same day or the next morning. Soft tissue injuries, concussions, and internal injuries don't always show symptoms at the scene. A gap in medical treatment gives insurers ammunition to argue your injuries weren't serious or weren't caused by the crash.

Document everything at the scene

If you're physically able: photograph both vehicles, the road conditions, any skid marks, traffic signals, and the surrounding intersection. Get the names, license plate numbers, and insurance information for both drivers. Get contact information from witnesses. All of this is harder to reconstruct later.

Get the police report

If Houston Police Department or Harris County Sheriff's Office responded, a crash report was filed. You can request it through the Texas Department of Transportation's TxDOT Crash Report portal. The report documents the responding officer's assessment of fault, which matters when insurers dispute liability.

Be careful what you say to adjusters

Both insurers will call you quickly. They're friendly. They sound helpful. They want a recorded statement. You are not required to give one, especially not before you've spoken to an attorney. What you say in those early calls can be used to minimize your claim. If an adjuster calls, you can confirm the basics (you were a passenger, you were injured) and tell them you'll be in touch once you've consulted with counsel.

The Two-Year Clock in Texas

Texas gives most personal injury victims two years from the date of the accident to file a lawsuit. That's the statute of limitations under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Miss that deadline and you generally lose your right to sue, no matter how strong your case is.

Two years sounds like a lot of time. It goes fast when you're recovering, dealing with medical appointments, and waiting on insurance negotiations. The practical reality: evidence degrades, witnesses forget details, and building a strong case takes time. Most attorneys want to be involved well before the deadline, not racing toward it.

There are limited exceptions, for minors, for cases involving government vehicles, and a few others, but don't assume an exception applies to you. Talk to an attorney early.

Rideshare Crashes: Uber and Lyft Passengers Have Additional Considerations

Houston has a large rideshare presence, and passenger injury claims involving Uber or Lyft add another layer of complexity. Both companies carry commercial liability policies, up to $1 million per occurrence when a driver has an active fare, but the coverage depends on exactly what phase of the trip the driver was in at the time of the crash.

If your Lyft driver caused the crash, Lyft's commercial policy is the primary target. If a third-party driver hit your Lyft, you have claims against that driver and potentially against Lyft's underinsured motorist coverage. The rideshare company's insurance adjusters handle these situations routinely. You probably don't.

Rideshare injury claims also involve preserving your trip records. Screenshot your app confirmation and trip details immediately. That documentation ties the driver to the platform at the time of the crash, which matters for coverage purposes.

What Damages Can You Recover as an Injured Passenger?

Texas law allows injured passengers to pursue the same categories of damages as any other injury victim.

Economic damages are the concrete financial losses: medical bills (past and future), lost wages if your injuries kept you from working, and costs of future care or rehabilitation.

Non-economic damages cover things that don't have an invoice: physical pain and suffering, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment of life, and in serious cases, disfigurement or physical impairment.

Texas does not cap non-economic damages in car accident cases (unlike medical malpractice cases, which have different rules). What you can recover depends on the specific facts of your injury, your treatment, and your life. Not on a formula.

Being a passenger actually puts you in a stronger legal position than either driver in most crashes. You didn't cause it. The fight over fault happens between the drivers, not between you and either of them.

When Does a Passenger Injury Claim Need an Attorney?

Not every crash requires legal representation. If you walked away with a minor scrape and your medical bills were handled quickly, you may not need one. But passenger injury claims involving more than minor injuries, especially when two drivers are disputing fault, almost always benefit from attorney involvement.

Here's why: you have claims against potentially two insurance companies, each of whom has adjusters and attorneys whose job is to pay as little as possible. You're managing your own recovery at the same time. An attorney handles the insurance communications, preserves evidence, coordinates your medical documentation, and negotiates across both policies. If a fair resolution isn't reached, they file suit in Harris County District Court on your behalf.

A Quick Summary: Your Rights as an Injured Passenger

  • You are almost never at fault as a passenger, and Texas law protects your right to full recovery.
  • You can file claims against both drivers simultaneously if both contributed to the crash.
  • You may have access to multiple insurance policies: both drivers' liability coverage, UM/UIM coverage, and your own health insurance.
  • You are not required to give a recorded statement to any insurance adjuster before consulting an attorney.
  • You have two years from the date of the crash to file suit under Texas law, but starting early protects your evidence and options.
  • Rideshare passengers have additional coverage layers but also additional complexity.
