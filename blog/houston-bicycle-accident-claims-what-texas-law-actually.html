Houston Bicycle Accident Claims: What Texas Law Actually Does for Cyclists

Bicyclist fatalities in Texas rose 58% in just four years, and Harris County is one of the deadliest places in the state to ride. If a driver hit you, here's what your claim looks like under Texas law, why bias against cyclists is a real obstacle, and what to do before you talk to any insurance company.

By Vu Nguyen, Founder & Managing Partner

You were riding your bike. A driver ran a red light, drifted into the shoulder, or opened a car door into your path. Now you're in the emergency room at Memorial Hermann or Ben Taub with broken bones, road rash, or worse. Your phone is already lighting up with a call from an insurance adjuster.

What happens next matters a lot. Texas law gives injured cyclists real legal rights. But there's a gap between what the law says and what actually happens in a claim. That gap often comes down to one uncomfortable truth: a lot of insurance adjusters, defense lawyers, and even jurors start from the assumption that cyclists are partly to blame just for being on the road.

This post explains how Texas law treats bicycle accident claims, what tools you have when a driver hits you, and why cyclist bias is a documented problem you need to understand before you say anything to an adjuster.

Harris County Is One of the Most Dangerous Places in Texas to Ride

That's not an opinion. It comes from TxDOT's own crash data. Texas recorded a 58% increase in bicyclist fatalities between 2019 and 2023. Harris County, which includes Houston and its inner suburbs, consistently sits at or near the top of the statewide death count.

Cycling is growing in Houston. More people use bikes for commuting, recreation, and deliveries than ever before. The road infrastructure hasn't kept up. Protected bike lanes are sparse. Drivers along corridors like Westheimer, Navigation Boulevard, and Memorial Drive share lanes with cyclists but often don't treat them like they belong there.

When crashes happen, they're not minor fender-benders. A cyclist hit by a 4,000-pound vehicle at 35 mph absorbs massive force with no metal cage around them. Traumatic brain injuries, spinal fractures, and internal injuries are common even when the rider was wearing a helmet and doing everything right.

Your Basic Rights Under Texas Law

Under Texas law, a bicycle is a vehicle. The Texas Transportation Code treats cyclists with the same legal status as drivers on most public roads. You have the right to ride on the roadway. Drivers must give you space. They must yield when required. They cannot simply brush you off as an obstacle.

When a driver's negligence causes a crash, that driver can be held legally responsible for your injuries. Running a stop sign, speeding, texting, failing to check mirrors before opening a door. The legal theory is straightforward: negligence. The driver owed you a duty of care, they breached it, and you got hurt because of it.

What makes bicycle cases complicated isn't the basic law. It's the fault fight that follows.

Texas Comparative Fault. and Why It Matters So Much for Cyclists

Texas uses a system called modified comparative fault. Your recovery gets reduced by whatever percentage of fault is assigned to you. If a jury decides you were 20% at fault for the crash, you recover 80% of your total damages. If they decide you were 51% or more at fault, you recover nothing at all.

That 51% threshold is the knife's edge in bicycle cases. Insurance adjusters know it. They look for any reason to push your fault percentage above 50%, because at that point, they owe you zero.

Common arguments adjusters and defense lawyers use to shift blame onto cyclists:

  • You weren't wearing a helmet (even though Texas law doesn't require adults to wear helmets)
  • You were riding at night without lights
  • You were riding on a road where cyclists "shouldn't" be, even if it was legal
  • You failed to signal a turn
  • You were in the driver's blind spot

Some of those might be legitimate facts. Some are just noise designed to make you seem reckless. The key is understanding that in Texas, partial fault doesn't automatically end your case. It reduces it. The fight is over how much fault you bear, and that fight starts the moment the adjuster picks up the phone.

An adjuster's first call isn't a courtesy check. It's a data-gathering mission. What you say in that first conversation can be used to push your fault percentage up, which directly reduces what the insurer has to pay you.

The Houston VRU Ordinance. Extra Protection You May Not Know About

Houston has its own local ordinance that provides additional protections for Vulnerable Road Users (VRUs), a category that includes cyclists, pedestrians, and others without the protection of a vehicle frame around them.

Under Houston's VRU ordinance, drivers must give cyclists at least three feet of clearance when passing. Violation of that rule is evidence of negligence. If a driver passed you closer than three feet and clipped you, that specific violation can be powerful evidence in your claim. It goes beyond general "careless driving" to a concrete, measurable breach of a local safety rule.

Keep this in mind when you're documenting the crash. If witnesses saw the driver pass close, or if there's dashcam or traffic camera footage from somewhere like the intersection at Montrose and Allen Parkway, that footage could show the spacing violation directly.

Hit-and-Run? You Still Have Options.

Hit-and-run bicycle crashes happen more often than most people realize. A driver clips a cyclist and keeps going. Sometimes it's panic. Sometimes it's intentional. Either way, you're left with real injuries and no one to sue directly.

Here's what a lot of cyclists don't know: your own auto insurance, specifically uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage, may cover you even though you were on a bicycle, not in a car.

UM/UIM coverage is designed to protect you when the at-fault driver has no insurance or can't be identified. Under Texas law, if you have auto insurance with UM/UIM coverage, that policy generally follows you as a person, not just when you're driving. A hit-and-run while you were on your bike may trigger that coverage.

There are conditions and limits, and every policy is different. But if you've been in a hit-and-run, the first call you make should be to an attorney, not the insurance company. Understand what coverage you have before you say anything that could affect your claim.

Also worth checking: your homeowner's or renter's insurance policy. Some include personal liability or medical payments coverage that can apply to bicycle accident injuries.

The Bias Problem Is Real. Here's What It Looks Like

Texas law treats cyclists as legitimate road users. Juries and adjusters don't always follow the law's lead.

Studies on mock jury behavior in bicycle accident cases consistently find that jurors assign more blame to cyclists than the evidence warrants, particularly when the cyclist was riding in a lane with vehicle traffic rather than on a dedicated path. The underlying assumption: cyclists take a risk by riding on roads with cars, so they accept some responsibility for whatever happens.

That assumption is legally wrong. Texas law doesn't work that way. But it's a real dynamic that experienced plaintiff attorneys deal with constantly.

What this means practically: how your case is built and presented matters enormously. Evidence that the driver was speeding, distracted, or violated a specific traffic rule helps shift the narrative. Witness statements collected quickly, before memories fade, help. Dashcam footage, if any exists, is gold. Medical records documenting the full extent of your injuries, gathered right away and not six weeks later, help too.

Cyclist bias is also why the recorded statement problem is so acute in these cases. When an adjuster gets you on the phone within hours of the crash, they're looking for anything you say that sounds like you weren't sure of your own position, speed, or actions. They write it down. They use it later. The best thing to say to an adjuster before you've spoken with an attorney is nothing at all.

What Damages Can You Recover?

Texas personal injury law allows injured cyclists to seek compensation for both economic and non-economic losses. Economic damages are the financial costs: medical bills, future medical care, lost wages if you couldn't work while you recovered, and lost earning capacity if the injury affects your ability to work long-term.

Non-economic damages cover the human losses: physical pain, emotional suffering, and loss of enjoyment of life. For serious bicycle injuries, a fractured pelvis, a traumatic brain injury, a spinal cord injury, non-economic damages can be substantial. These injuries don't just cost money. They change lives.

Texas does not cap non-economic damages in personal injury cases against private defendants (unlike medical malpractice, which has its own rules). That matters.

One thing to understand about property damage: your bicycle is covered separately from your personal injury claim. Document the damage thoroughly. Keep the bike. Don't throw it away or let the insurance company take it without documentation, because it's physical evidence of the force of the crash.

The Two-Year Clock

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, you generally have two years from the date of your bicycle accident to file a personal injury lawsuit. Miss that deadline and your claim is gone, regardless of how strong it was.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't. Evidence disappears. Witnesses move away. Traffic camera footage gets overwritten within days or weeks. The earlier you start building your case, the better your position.

There are limited exceptions to the two-year rule. If the injured person is a minor, for instance, the clock may be paused. But exceptions are narrow. Don't plan around them.

The statute of limitations isn't just a legal deadline. It's a practical one. The best evidence in your case exists right now, today, and it's disappearing fast.

Steps to Take Right Now If You Were Hit

If you haven't done these things yet, do them as soon as you're physically able:

  1. Get medical attention, and keep going. Go to the ER, urgent care, or your doctor. If you were taken to Ben Taub, Memorial Hermann, or Houston Methodist, get copies of your records. If symptoms get worse in the following days (headaches, dizziness, back pain), go back. Don't dismiss symptoms that show up after the adrenaline wears off.
  2. Document everything at the scene if you can. Photos of your bike, the vehicle, the road surface, skid marks, traffic signals, and your injuries. Get the driver's license plate, insurance information, and name. If witnesses are present, get their contact information.
  3. File a police report. In Houston, you can contact HPD or TxDOT depending on where the crash occurred. A police report creates an official record of the crash. It matters.
  4. Don't give the adjuster a recorded statement. You are not legally required to give a recorded statement to the other driver's insurance company. Politely decline until you've spoken with an attorney.
  5. Keep your bike and all gear. Don't repair or dispose of anything until your attorney has seen it. The physical evidence tells a story.
  6. Write down everything you remember. As soon as you can, write a detailed account of what happened: where you were, what direction you were going, what the driver did, weather conditions, time of day. Memory fades fast.

When You Need a Houston Bicycle Accident Lawyer

Not every crash requires an attorney. If you have minor injuries, the other driver clearly was at fault, and the insurance company is cooperating, you might handle it yourself.

But in most serious bicycle accident cases, an attorney makes a real difference. Here's why.

Bicycle cases are fault fights. The comparative fault system means the insurance company has a financial incentive to push your share of blame as high as possible. An attorney who handles Houston injury cases knows those tactics and knows how to counter them with evidence.

UM/UIM claims after hit-and-runs have their own procedural requirements under Texas law. Getting those wrong can waive coverage you're entitled to.

Serious injuries, brain injuries, spinal injuries, fractures that require surgery, involve future damages that are genuinely complex to calculate. Medical experts, life care planners, and economists sometimes have to get involved. An adjuster's early offer almost never accounts for those future costs accurately.
