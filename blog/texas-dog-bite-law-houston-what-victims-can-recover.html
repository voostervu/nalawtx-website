Texas Dog Bite Law: Why the 'One Free Bite' Rule Doesn't Always Protect the Owner

Texas is a one-bite state, but that label is misleading. Dog owners can be held liable even when their pet has never attacked before. Here's what Houston victims actually need to know.

Texas ranks second in the country for dog attacks. Houston consistently lands among the most dangerous cities for postal workers, joggers, and kids in their own neighborhoods. If a dog bit you, or someone you love, the first thing you'll probably hear from the owner is some version of "he's never done that before."

That defense has a name. It's called the one-bite rule. And while it's real Texas law, it's nowhere near the complete shield that dog owners think it is. Victims have multiple paths to compensation, and a few of them don't require proving anything about the dog's history at all.

What the One-Bite Rule Actually Says

Texas doesn't have a statewide dog bite statute the way California or Florida does. Instead, Texas relies on common law, court-made rules developed over decades of cases. Under that framework, an owner is liable if they knew (or should have known) their dog had dangerous propensities.

The "one free bite" shorthand comes from this idea: if a dog has bitten someone before, the owner now has knowledge of the danger. That prior bite is evidence of what the owner knew. But calling it a "free bite" misrepresents how courts actually apply the rule.

Prior biting isn't the only way to prove an owner knew their dog was dangerous. Growling at strangers, lunging on a leash, snapping without contact, a breed history of aggression. courts have accepted all of these as evidence of known dangerous propensities.

In short, if your neighbor's dog has been lunging at kids on the sidewalk for two years but never made contact, and then it bites your child, the owner doesn't get a free pass just because teeth never broke skin before.

Negligence: The Second Route to Liability

You don't have to win the "dangerous propensity" argument to hold an owner responsible. Texas recognizes a separate negligence claim that works differently.

Negligence here means the owner failed to use reasonable care to control the dog, regardless of what the dog had done in the past. Did the owner let a large, excitable dog run loose in a crowded area? Did they fail to latch a gate? Did they walk the dog off-leash near a school on Westheimer Road? Those choices can be negligent even if the dog had the friendliest history imaginable.

To win on negligence, the victim generally needs to show four things:

  1. The owner had a duty to control the dog
  2. They breached that duty through careless behavior
  3. That breach caused the attack
  4. The victim suffered real injuries as a result

This path matters especially in cases involving large breeds, unleashed dogs, or situations where the owner was clearly not paying attention. The dog's history becomes less central to the case.

Negligence Per Se: When a Local Law Creates Automatic Liability

Houston and Harris County have leash laws and animal control ordinances. When a dog owner violates one of those ordinances, say, their dog is running loose in violation of a City of Houston ordinance, and someone gets hurt as a result, the victim may have a negligence per se claim.

Negligence per se means the violation of the law itself establishes the breach of duty. You don't have to argue what "reasonable care" looks like when there's a specific rule the owner ignored. The broken law does that work for you.

This is one of the most underused angles in Houston dog bite cases. A lot of victims don't realize that the same ordinance a Houston Animal Control officer might cite the owner for can also support a civil injury claim.

Lillian's Law and Criminal Liability

Texas added a criminal dimension to severe dog attacks through what's commonly called Lillian's Law, named after a 76-year-old woman killed by a neighbor's dogs in 2007. The law is codified in the Texas Health & Safety Code and applies when a dog that has been previously declared dangerous, or whose owner was previously found criminally negligent, attacks and causes serious bodily injury or death.

Under this law, a dog owner can face criminal charges ranging from a Class C misdemeanor to a second-degree felony depending on how severe the outcome is and what the owner knew or did. A second-degree felony in Texas carries 2 to 20 years in prison.

For victims, Lillian's Law matters for two reasons. First, a criminal conviction creates a record that can be powerful evidence in a civil case. Second, it signals to insurers that the exposure is serious, which can affect how aggressively they negotiate.

If your attack involved a dog that Animal Control had flagged, a prior dangerous dog declaration, or an owner who knew about past incidents, make sure your attorney knows about the criminal law angle early.

Landlord Liability: A Path That Surprises People

A lot of Houston dog attacks happen in apartment complexes, rented houses, and other properties where the dog owner is a tenant. The tenant may have little money or no renters insurance. That doesn't mean you're out of options.

Landlords can be liable for dog attacks in certain situations. If a landlord knew a tenant kept a dangerous dog on the property and did nothing, didn't prohibit the dog, didn't enforce a lease provision, didn't take steps to remove the animal, the landlord may share responsibility for what happened.

Texas courts have recognized landlord liability in dog bite cases where the landlord had actual knowledge of the dog's dangerous nature and had the ability to require the tenant to remove the animal. This doesn't apply to every apartment attack, but it's worth investigating when the tenant-owner has no meaningful assets or coverage.

If you were attacked at a complex near the Medical Center, or in a rental property in Pasadena or Katy, ask your attorney whether the property owner knew about the dog's behavior before the attack.

What Damages Can a Houston Dog Bite Victim Pursue?

Dog attacks cause serious injuries: deep lacerations, nerve damage, broken bones from falls, and in attacks on children, permanent scarring on their faces and hands. The emotional aftermath can be just as lasting. Many victims develop genuine anxiety around dogs and in some cases post-traumatic stress disorder.

In a Texas civil claim, victims typically pursue:

  • Medical expenses. emergency care at Ben Taub, Memorial Hermann, or wherever you were treated; follow-up visits; physical therapy; reconstructive surgery
  • Lost income. wages missed while recovering, or reduced earning capacity if injuries affect your ability to work
  • Pain and suffering. physical pain from the attack and recovery period
  • Mental anguish. anxiety, fear, PTSD, and psychological trauma that follows the attack
  • Disfigurement. permanent scarring, especially significant in cases involving children or visible areas like the face, neck, or hands

Texas also allows punitive damages, called exemplary damages under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 41.001, in cases involving gross negligence or malice. These are awarded on top of actual damages when the owner's conduct was especially reckless. A dog owner who sicced a dog on someone intentionally, or who ignored repeated warnings about a dangerous animal, could face exemplary damages.

The Two-Year Deadline You Cannot Miss

Texas personal injury claims, including dog bites, are governed by a two-year statute of limitations under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. That means you have two years from the date of the attack to file a lawsuit.

Two years sounds like plenty of time. It goes fast. Evidence disappears. Animal Control records get purged. Witnesses move. Wounds heal in ways that make injuries harder to document. The sooner you start building your case, the stronger it will be.

Cases involving minors have a different timeline. The two-year clock typically doesn't start until the child turns 18. But don't rely on that without talking to an attorney first, because there are exceptions and complications that vary by situation.

The worst thing a dog bite victim in Houston can do is wait to see if the owner's homeowners insurance "takes care of it." Insurance companies aren't on your side. They're managing their exposure. Get independent legal advice before you sign anything or give a recorded statement.

What to Do Right After a Dog Attack in Houston

If you were just attacked, or if this happened recently, here's what helps your case:

  1. Get medical attention immediately. Dog bites carry infection risk including rabies. Go to an emergency room or urgent care. Your medical records are the foundation of your injury claim.
  2. Report the attack to Houston Animal Control. Call 311 or go online. A formal report creates an official record and may trigger a dangerous dog investigation. That record can matter a great deal in your civil case.
  3. Document the scene and your injuries. Photograph wounds before they're cleaned, if possible. Get photos of the location, any broken fencing or open gates, and the dog if you can do so safely. Take pictures of your injuries every few days as they heal.
  4. Get the owner's information. Name, address, phone number, and their homeowners or renters insurance if they'll provide it.
  5. Find out if the dog has a history. Ask neighbors. Check with Animal Control. Prior complaints about the same dog become part of your case.
  6. Talk to an attorney before talking to the owner's insurance. The adjuster who calls you is not trying to help you. They're looking for statements they can use to reduce what you recover.

How Homeowners Insurance Usually Fits In

Most dog bite claims in Texas run through the dog owner's homeowners or renters insurance policy. Standard homeowners policies cover dog bite liability, though some insurers exclude certain breeds or dogs with a documented bite history.

The insurer will assign an adjuster to your claim. That adjuster may contact you quickly, sound friendly and helpful, and offer a fast settlement. Early settlement offers are almost always lower than what a fully documented claim is worth. Once you sign a release, you give up your right to pursue additional compensation, even if you find out later that your injuries are more serious than they first appeared.

If the owner has no insurance, or if the policy limits are too low for the severity of your injuries, an attorney can help identify other sources of recovery, including landlord liability if applicable, or uninsured/underinsured options depending on your own policy coverage.
