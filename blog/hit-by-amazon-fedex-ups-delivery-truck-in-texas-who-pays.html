Hit by an Amazon, FedEx, or UPS Truck in Texas? Who Actually Pays

The logo on the side of the van is not always the company that pays your claim. Who is responsible depends on which delivery network the driver works in, and each one is set up differently.

By Vu Nguyen, Founder & Managing Partner · Published November 26, 2026

If a delivery van hits you in Texas, the company whose logo is on the van may or may not be legally responsible. Amazon, FedEx Ground, and Amazon Flex use contractor networks; UPS and FedEx Express mostly use their own employees; and U.S. Postal Service crashes follow federal rules. Identifying the right company, and every insurance policy that applies, is the first and most important step in your claim.

  • Most Amazon-branded vans are driven by employees of separate Delivery Service Partner companies.
  • Amazon Flex coverage applies only while the driver is actively on a delivery block.
  • FedEx Ground uses independent contractor companies; FedEx Express and UPS mostly use employees.
  • A USPS crash requires a federal administrative claim within two years before you can sue.

Delivery vans are everywhere in Texas neighborhoods now. They stop suddenly, double-park, back out of driveways, and run tight routes against the clock. When one of them hits you, most people assume the claim is simple: Amazon (or FedEx, or UPS) is a huge company, so Amazon pays.

It is rarely that simple. These companies have built their delivery networks in very different ways, and those choices decide who you can hold responsible and how much insurance is actually available. Here is how each one works, in plain language.

Why the logo on the van doesn't settle the question

Texas law generally makes an employer responsible for the negligence of its employees while they are doing their jobs. That rule is called respondeat superior, or vicarious liability. But it usually does not apply to independent contractors, because the hiring company does not control how the contractor does the work.

That distinction is the whole game in delivery truck cases. If the driver is the big company's employee, the big company is typically responsible. If the driver works for a separate contractor company, the big company will argue it is not responsible at all, and you may be left with a small company and a much smaller insurance policy unless your lawyer can show more.

The first question in every delivery truck case is not "how bad was the crash?" It's "who did this driver really work for, and who controlled the work?"

Amazon: three different setups

1. Amazon Delivery Service Partners (the blue Amazon vans)

Most of the Amazon-branded vans you see are operated by Delivery Service Partners (DSPs). These are separate small businesses that contract with Amazon and employ the drivers directly. The driver's paycheck comes from the DSP, not from Amazon.

After a crash, Amazon typically takes the position that the DSP, not Amazon, is responsible for its drivers. But that is not the end of the analysis. Courts look at who had the right to control the work. Evidence that Amazon set the routes, the number of stops, the delivery pace, the training, the monitoring, and the discipline can support a claim that reaches Amazon itself. Amazon's own policies, such as unrealistic delivery quotas that push drivers to rush, can also support a direct negligence claim.

Your attorney should also get the DSP's full insurance policy, not just a summary page, to see whether Amazon is listed as an additional insured, and review any contract terms that shift responsibility between the two companies.

2. Amazon Flex (personal cars, often no logo at all)

Amazon Flex drivers are independent contractors who deliver in their own cars. According to Amazon, it provides commercial auto insurance, including liability coverage, to Flex delivery partners in Texas, but only while they are actively delivering during a scheduled delivery block.

That timing detail matters. If the Flex driver was between blocks, running a personal errand, or already finished for the day, Amazon's coverage may not apply, and the driver's personal auto policy (which may exclude delivery work) becomes the main source. Screenshots of the delivery app, the time of the crash, and the driver's own statements at the scene can make or break this issue.

3. Amazon's own tractor-trailers

Larger Amazon trailers moving freight between warehouses on highways like I-10 and I-45 may be run by Amazon's own trucking operations or by outside freight carriers. These are commercial motor vehicles subject to federal trucking rules, and they typically carry much larger insurance policies. The same questions apply: who owned the truck, who employed the driver, and who controlled the trip.

FedEx: Express vs. Ground makes a big difference

FedEx runs two very different delivery systems.

  • FedEx Express drivers are generally FedEx employees. If an Express driver causes a crash while on the job, FedEx is typically responsible under respondeat superior.
  • FedEx Ground (including Home Delivery) uses independent service providers: separate companies that own or lease the trucks and employ their own drivers. FedEx Ground often argues that the contractor, not FedEx, is responsible for those drivers.

In a FedEx Ground case, your attorney will want the contractor agreement, the contractor's insurance policies, and evidence of how much FedEx controlled the routes, schedules, equipment, and driver standards. Many FedEx Ground trucks are over 10,000 pounds, which can also bring federal motor carrier safety rules and higher insurance requirements into play.

UPS: usually the simplest of the three

UPS package car drivers (the brown trucks) are generally UPS employees. That usually makes the liability question more straightforward: if the driver was negligent while making deliveries, UPS is typically responsible. UPS has its own claims operation and insurance program, and like every large company, it will investigate quickly and look for ways to shift blame to you.

USPS: federal rules and a special deadline

If a U.S. Postal Service vehicle hits you, the rules change completely. Claims against USPS fall under the Federal Tort Claims Act. Before you can file a lawsuit, you must submit a written administrative claim to USPS, usually on Standard Form 95, that states a specific dollar amount, within two years of the crash. You generally cannot sue until USPS denies the claim or six months pass without a decision. Federal cases are tried to a judge, not a jury.

Some mail in Texas is also delivered by private contractors under USPS contracts, and those cases may follow the normal Texas rules instead. Identifying who actually owned and operated the vehicle comes first.

A USPS claim with the wrong dollar amount, or filed after two years, can be fatal to your case. This is one of the few situations where the paperwork itself can decide the outcome.

How much insurance is really available?

Texas only requires drivers to carry $30,000 per person and $60,000 per crash in bodily injury liability coverage (Texas Transportation Code § 601.072). For a serious injury, that does not go far.

Delivery companies and their contractors usually carry more than the state minimum, and interstate for-hire carriers operating vehicles over 10,000 pounds must meet federal minimum insurance levels. But many delivery vans fall under that weight line, and small contractor companies may carry only what their contract requires. That is why finding every policy matters: the contractor's policy, any policy that names the larger company as an insured, umbrella or excess policies, and your own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage.

What to do after a delivery truck hits you

Photograph the vehicle from every side. Capture the company name, any DSP or contractor name on the door, the license plate, and any USDOT number. Contractor names are often small and easy to miss.

Get the driver's information and ask who they work for. Write down exactly what they say. "I drive for Amazon" can mean a DSP employee, a Flex contractor, or a freight carrier.

Call 911 and get a police report. The report will usually identify the vehicle owner and insurer, which is often the fastest way to identify the right company.

Get medical care right away and keep every record and bill. Gaps in treatment are one of the first things a corporate insurer will use against you.

Don't give a recorded statement to the delivery company or its insurer before you talk to a lawyer. You are not required to, and their adjusters are trained to look for anything that shifts fault to you.

Act quickly on evidence. Delivery vehicles often have telematics, GPS, and cameras, and companies track every stop. That data can show speed, braking, and whether the driver was rushing. It may not be kept forever, so your attorney should send a preservation letter early.

Fault still matters

Texas uses a modified comparative fault rule (Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 33.001). If you are found partly at fault, your recovery is reduced by your percentage. If you are found more than 50% at fault, you recover nothing. Delivery companies know this, and they will argue you were speeding, distracted, or should have seen the van stopped in the road.

Common questions

Can I sue Amazon if an Amazon delivery van hits me?

Often you can bring a claim that reaches Amazon, but it depends on who was driving. Most Amazon-branded vans are operated by Delivery Service Partners (DSPs), separate companies that employ the drivers. Amazon usually argues it is not responsible for the DSP's drivers. Your attorney can still pursue Amazon where evidence shows Amazon controlled the driver's routes, pace, training, or discipline, or where Amazon's own policies contributed to the crash. The DSP's insurance and any policy that lists Amazon as an additional insured should also be examined.

What if the driver was an Amazon Flex driver in a personal car?

Amazon says it provides commercial auto insurance, including liability coverage, to Flex delivery partners in Texas, but only while they are actively delivering during a scheduled delivery block. If the driver was between blocks or had finished deliveries, that coverage may not apply and the driver's personal policy becomes the main source. That is why the exact time of the crash matters.

Is FedEx responsible if a FedEx truck hits me?

It depends on which FedEx company was involved. FedEx Express drivers are generally FedEx employees, so FedEx is typically responsible for their on-the-job negligence. FedEx Ground packages are delivered by independent service providers, separate companies that employ their own drivers. FedEx Ground often argues those contractors, not FedEx, are responsible, so the contracts, the contractor's insurance, and FedEx's level of control all become important.

How long do I have to file a claim after a delivery truck crash in Texas?

For most delivery truck crashes, Texas gives you two years from the date of the crash to file a lawsuit under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. If a U.S. Postal Service vehicle hit you, the claim falls under the Federal Tort Claims Act, and you must first file a written administrative claim (usually Standard Form 95) with USPS within two years. Missing either deadline can end your claim.
