Houston Nursing Home Abuse: What Texas Law Lets Families Recover

Texas classifies many nursing home claims as medical malpractice, triggering expert report deadlines most families never see coming. This post breaks down the rules, the warning signs, and what your family can actually pursue.

Your mother moved into a Houston-area nursing facility because she needed care you couldn't safely provide at home. Six months later, she has unexplained bruises, has lost 20 pounds, and flinches when a staff member walks into her room. You know something is wrong. What you may not know is that the legal path forward is more complicated, and more time-sensitive, than most families realize.

Texas has specific laws governing nursing home and assisted living claims. Some of those laws protect residents. Others, if you miss them, can end your family's case before it starts. Understanding both sides matters before you take a single step.

The Scale of the Problem in Texas

Texas has more than 4.8 million residents aged 65 and older. That number keeps climbing, and the elder care industry has grown to match it. Harris County alone has hundreds of licensed nursing facilities, assisted living communities, and memory care centers.

In 2025, Texas Adult Protective Services confirmed nearly 360,000 elder abuse cases statewide. That figure, from APS's own data, covers physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, financial exploitation, and neglect. Neglect is the most commonly confirmed type.

These are not rare or isolated events. Understaffed facilities, high employee turnover, and inadequate training create conditions where residents get hurt, sometimes severely. Bedsores that go untreated. Falls that happen because no one answered a call light. Medication errors that nobody documents. Residents who can no longer advocate for themselves, left in rooms where nothing gets reported.

What Counts as Abuse or Neglect

Texas law defines these terms broadly. Under the Texas Human Resources Code, abuse includes physical harm inflicted by a caregiver, sexual contact without consent, and emotional or verbal abuse. Neglect covers a caregiver's failure to provide food, water, shelter, medication, or medical care when that failure causes harm or puts a resident at substantial risk of harm.

In practical terms, families typically encounter several patterns:

  • Physical abuse. unexplained bruising, broken bones, injuries inconsistent with the explanation offered by staff
  • Neglect. pressure ulcers (bedsores) that develop because a resident isn't being repositioned, dehydration, malnutrition, poor hygiene
  • Medication errors. wrong drug, wrong dose, missed doses, or medications given to the wrong resident
  • Fall injuries. inadequate supervision, missing bed rails, wet floors, failure to follow a fall-prevention care plan
  • Emotional abuse. staff who berate, threaten, or isolate residents
  • Financial exploitation. a separate civil and criminal track; we'll note it here but it requires its own analysis

Not every bad outcome is abuse or neglect. Residents in nursing facilities are often medically fragile. Some decline despite good care. But when harm results from what a facility did, or failed to do, Texas law gives families options.

The Chapter 74 Problem: When Texas Treats This as Medical Malpractice

This is where most families get blindsided. It's also what makes nursing home cases genuinely different from other personal injury claims.

Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code Chapter 74 governs medical malpractice claims. When a nursing home provides services that constitute "health care". things like wound care, medication management, fall prevention protocols. a claim based on those services may be classified as a health care liability claim under Chapter 74, not a standard negligence claim.

That classification triggers two requirements most people don't know exist.

The Expert Report Deadline

Under Chapter 74, within 120 days of filing suit, you must serve an expert report on every defendant. The report has to come from a qualified medical professional. It must set out the standard of care the facility was supposed to meet, how the facility breached that standard, and how that breach caused the resident's injuries.

Miss that 120-day window, even by one day, and the defendant can ask the court to dismiss your case and award them attorney's fees. Courts have very limited discretion to extend the deadline. In practice, a missed expert report deadline usually ends the case.

That's not a technicality. It's a real cliff that has ended legitimate claims by families who didn't know it existed.

The Statute of Limitations

Standard personal injury claims in Texas generally carry a two-year statute of limitations under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003. Health care liability claims under Chapter 74 also carry a two-year limit, but the clock can start differently, and there are specific discovery-rule nuances for cases involving fraudulent concealment of injuries. For deceased residents, survival and wrongful death claims have their own timing considerations.

The point: there is no safe assumption about when time runs out. The facts of your specific situation determine the deadline, and getting that wrong is irreversible.

The 120-day expert report requirement under Chapter 74 has ended more legitimate nursing home cases than most people realize. Families lose their rights not because they waited too long to call, but because no one told them this rule existed.

Not All Nursing Home Claims Fall Under Chapter 74

Here's the other side of that: not every claim against a nursing home is automatically a medical malpractice claim. Texas courts have spent years sorting out where Chapter 74 applies and where it doesn't.

If the claim is based on the facility's failure as a business operator, security failures, physical plant hazards, negligent hiring of staff with known histories of abuse, courts have sometimes found those claims fall outside Chapter 74. That means no expert report requirement and a potentially simpler path to trial.

The distinction matters enormously. A resident sexually abused by a staff member who had prior misconduct on his record, and whom the facility hired anyway, may look very different legally from a claim about improper wound care protocol.

Figuring out which track applies requires analyzing the specific facts of what happened. This is not something to guess at. The wrong classification can either cost your case its procedural footing or cause you to over-build a case around requirements that don't actually apply.

What Families Can Recover

If a nursing home claim succeeds, Texas law allows families to pursue several categories of damages.

For Injured Residents Who Are Still Living

  • Medical expenses. past and future costs to treat the injuries caused by abuse or neglect
  • Pain and suffering. physical pain and emotional distress the resident experienced
  • Mental anguish. Texas law recognizes this as a separate category of harm
  • Disfigurement. relevant in cases involving severe pressure wounds or scarring
  • Physical impairment. if the abuse or neglect caused lasting functional loss

When a Resident Dies

Texas law allows two types of claims after a resident dies from nursing home abuse or neglect.

A survival claim is brought on behalf of the resident's estate. It covers the harm the resident personally experienced, their pain, their suffering, their medical costs, before death.

A wrongful death claim is brought by eligible family members: spouses, children, and parents. It covers the loss those family members suffered, grief, loss of companionship, loss of financial support.

Both claims can often be pursued at the same time. They serve different purposes and compensate for different harms.

Exemplary Damages

Texas law permits exemplary damages, sometimes called punitive damages, in cases involving malice, fraud, or gross negligence. Gross negligence means the defendant's conduct involved an extreme degree of risk, and they proceeded with conscious indifference to the rights and welfare of others.

A nursing home that knew a staff member was abusing residents and concealed it. A facility that repeatedly ignored documented bedsores. Cases with that kind of deliberate or reckless disregard can support an exemplary damages claim, which goes beyond compensating the family and is meant to punish the wrongdoer.

Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 41.008 caps exemplary damages in most civil cases. The cap calculation depends on the facts of the case. The cap does not apply to intentional conduct or certain other categories, which is another reason the specific classification of the claim matters.

What Texas Regulators Can and Cannot Do

Families often report nursing home abuse to the Texas Health and Human Services Commission (HHSC), which licenses and inspects facilities. They may also contact Adult Protective Services or local law enforcement. Those are the right steps, and they can trigger investigations, citations, and even license revocations.

But regulatory action and a civil lawsuit are separate tracks.

HHSC can fine a facility or require a corrective action plan. It cannot compensate your family. An APS substantiation of abuse confirms something happened, but APS doesn't file a lawsuit on your behalf. Criminal charges, if pursued by the Harris County District Attorney's office, address punishment of the abuser, not compensation for your family.

If your family wants compensation for what happened to your loved one, that requires a separate civil case. The regulatory findings can be powerful evidence in that case, but they don't replace it.

Warning Signs Families Often Miss

Nursing facilities are skilled at limiting information flow. Staff are often coached on what to say, and what not to say, when something goes wrong. Some warning signs families don't always connect to abuse or neglect:

  • Staff who are evasive when you ask how an injury happened
  • Records that seem to appear or change after an incident
  • A facility that discourages unannounced visits
  • Residents who become withdrawn or fearful of specific staff members
  • Repeated infections, UTIs, pneumonia, that suggest inadequate hygiene protocols
  • Sudden or unexplained changes in a resident's mental or emotional state
  • Weight loss that staff attribute only to the resident's underlying condition

Trust your instincts. If something feels wrong, start documenting everything: dates, staff names, what was said, photographs of any visible injuries. That record becomes evidence.

What to Do Right Now If You Suspect Abuse

If your loved one is in immediate danger, call 911. Physical safety comes first.

If the situation is serious but not an emergency, contact the Texas HHSC complaint line at 1-800-458-9858. You can report nursing home neglect or abuse 24 hours a day.

Preserve records. Request copies of the facility's incident reports, care plans, and medication administration records. Texas law gives you rights to those records. The facility cannot lawfully deny access to a resident's authorized representative.

Photograph injuries as soon as you see them. Document the date, time, and location. If a resident can speak and is willing to describe what happened, note that too.

Then talk to an attorney before you say anything further to the facility or its insurer. Nursing home companies carry liability insurance, and their adjusters handle these calls all day. Your family is not in an equal position in that conversation without legal guidance.

The 120-day expert report clock starts running once suit is filed. The two-year limitations period runs before suit must be filed. Time is a real factor. An attorney handling nursing home and elder abuse cases can assess quickly whether Chapter 74 applies and what deadlines govern your situation.

Families who wait to see how things develop sometimes run out of legal options before they realize the clock was running. If you suspect abuse, a consultation costs nothing and tells you where you stand.

Choosing an Attorney for a Houston Nursing Home Case

Nursing home litigation in Texas is genuinely specialized. It sits at the intersection of elder law, medical malpractice procedure, and personal injury. Not every personal injury attorney has handled it.

Ask the attorney you consult with whether they have experience specifically with Chapter 74 health care liability claims. Ask whether they work with qualified medical experts who can produce the required expert report. Ask how they assess whether a claim falls inside or outside Chapter 74, because the answer matters to your case strategy.
