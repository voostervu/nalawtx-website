The Trucker's Logbook That Could Win Your Case. and Why You Have Six Months to Get It

If a fatigued 18-wheeler driver hit you in Texas, federal records prove how long that driver had been behind the wheel. But those records disappear after six months. Here's what hours-of-service violations mean for your claim, and exactly what needs to happen right now.

Picture a loaded semi rolling south on I-45 toward Houston at 2 a.m. The driver has been on the road for 13 hours. Federal law says he should have stopped at 11. Nobody flagged it. His company needed the delivery on time. And then he drifted into your lane near the Beltway 8 interchange.

The crash report will say "driver error." What it won't say, unless someone digs for it, is that the driver was operating illegally, in violation of federal hours-of-service rules that exist for one reason: to keep exhausted truckers off the road. That distinction matters enormously for your claim. It's the difference between a standard negligence case and one where the trucker's employer may have handed you proof of fault on a silver platter.

What Hours-of-Service Rules Actually Say

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (the FMCSA, the federal agency that regulates commercial trucking) sets mandatory limits on how long a truck driver can work before stopping to rest. These aren't suggestions. They're federal law, codified in 49 C.F.R. Part 395.

For property-carrying drivers (the 18-wheelers you see on I-10 and the Gulf Freeway every day), the core limits are:

  • 11-hour driving limit: A driver may not drive more than 11 hours after 10 consecutive hours off duty.
  • 14-hour on-duty window: A driver may not drive beyond the 14th hour after coming on duty, even if they took breaks during that time.
  • 30-minute break requirement: After 8 cumulative hours of driving, a driver must take a 30-minute break.
  • 60/70-hour weekly limit: A driver can't drive after accumulating 60 hours on duty in 7 consecutive days, or 70 hours in 8 days.

Every one of those rules has a paper trail. And increasingly, a digital one.

ELDs: The Black Box in the Cab

Since December 2017, most commercial carriers operating in interstate commerce have been required to equip their trucks with an Electronic Logging Device, or ELD. An ELD automatically records driving time by connecting directly to the truck's engine. It's tamper-resistant by design. The driver can't conveniently "forget" to log a stretch of highway the way they could with an old paper logbook.

ELD data captures when the engine was running, the vehicle's speed and location at regular intervals, when the driver switched duty status, and whether any manual edits were made to the record. In a crash case, this data is extraordinarily powerful. It shows a court exactly how long that driver was moving before impact.

ELD data doesn't lie. A paper logbook can be falsified. An ELD connected to the engine's ECM records what the truck actually did, minute by minute.

There's one critical catch: under FMCSA regulations, carriers are only required to retain ELD records for six months. After that window closes, the data may be gone. Trucking companies know this. Some don't go out of their way to preserve records once the retention period expires.

Six months sounds like a long time. It isn't. Between recovering from your injuries, dealing with insurance calls, and figuring out what happened, that window closes fast.

What "Negligence Per Se" Means for Your Claim

In a standard negligence case, you have to show four things: the defendant owed you a duty, they breached it, the breach caused your injuries, and you suffered real damages. Proving breach, that the driver actually did something wrong, takes work.

Hours-of-service violations can change that through a legal theory called negligence per se. Here's the plain-English version: when a defendant violates a safety statute or regulation designed to prevent the kind of harm you suffered, Texas courts can treat that violation as automatic evidence of negligence. You don't have to separately argue that driving fatigued was unreasonable. The law already decided it was.

Texas courts have applied this doctrine in trucking cases where FMCSA hours-of-service regulations were violated. The regulation exists to protect other drivers on the road. You're a driver on the road who got hurt. The logic is direct.

That doesn't mean you win automatically. You still have to prove the violation happened and connect it to your crash. But it shifts the conversation significantly, especially in front of a Houston jury that understands what it's like to share the road with an 80,000-pound truck.

Paper Logs, Falsified Records, and What to Look For

Not every truck is equipped with a compliant ELD. Older vehicles, short-haul exemptions, and some agricultural haulers still use paper logs. And even with ELDs in place, fraud happens. Drivers operating for high-pressure carriers sometimes manipulate their duty status, logging themselves as "off duty" or "in the sleeper berth" while still driving, or split driving time across multiple logbooks.

Signs of a falsified or suspicious logbook include:

  • Logs showing perfectly round-numbered hours that never vary
  • Driving times that don't match fuel receipts, toll records, or GPS data
  • Missing logs for dates before the crash
  • Edits to electronic records made after the fact, with no legitimate explanation
  • Discrepancies between the ELD data and what the driver reported to police

An attorney working a truck accident claim will typically issue a preservation demand, a formal legal letter telling the trucking company to preserve all ELD data, paper logs, dispatch records, and communications related to the driver, within days of being retained. Without that letter, records that should exist may not be there when you need them.

The Carrier Can Be on the Hook Too

Trucking companies have their own federal obligations. They're required to audit driver logs, monitor hours compliance, and refuse to dispatch a driver who is over their hours limit. When a carrier ignores known violations, or builds a culture where drivers feel pressured to push past legal limits to meet delivery deadlines, the company itself can bear liability alongside the driver.

This matters because individual truck drivers often don't carry enough personal assets to fully compensate someone with serious injuries. The carrier, on the other hand, is required under federal law to carry substantial liability insurance: a minimum of $750,000 for most freight, and $1,000,000 or more for hazardous materials. Establishing the company's role in the violation is often central to making sure there's enough coverage to address what happened to you.

When a carrier's dispatch records show the driver was given an impossible schedule, that's not just driver error anymore. That's a company decision.

What a Dispatcher's Records Can Show

Dispatch logs and trip assignment records can reveal that the carrier knew, or should have known, the driver couldn't complete the route legally within their available hours. If a Houston-based carrier sent a driver from Dallas to Laredo and back with a 4-hour delivery window that only worked if the driver skipped their mandatory rest period, that's in the records. So are the communications: texts, load assignment apps, email threads.

These documents are also subject to the six-month (and sometimes shorter) retention cycles. The preservation demand has to cover all of it.

How This Plays Out in a Harris County Courtroom

Most truck accident claims in Houston fall under the jurisdiction of Harris County District Courts. If your case proceeds to litigation, the ELD data and logbook records become exhibits. An expert witness, often a former FMCSA compliance officer or a certified accident reconstructionist, will walk the jury through what the data shows.

Jurors in Harris County understand trucking. Houston is one of the largest freight hubs in the country. I-10, I-69, and the Port of Houston move an enormous volume of commercial traffic every day. That context cuts both ways: jurors understand the industry's pressures, but they also understand that a driver who's been behind the wheel for 13 hours is not safe at any speed.

Under Texas law, specifically the modified comparative fault rule, your recovery gets reduced by your own percentage of fault, if any. But a trucking company arguing that a driver who violated federal regulations is only partly to blame faces a difficult case. Documented hours-of-service violations are hard to explain away in front of a jury.

Texas's Two-Year Deadline, and Why You Shouldn't Wait That Long

Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, you generally have two years from the date of your injury to file a personal injury lawsuit. Two years feels like plenty of time until it isn't.

In truck accident cases specifically, the evidence clock runs faster than the legal clock. ELD data: six months. Some carriers purge dispatch records on similar cycles. Dashcam footage from the truck cab or trailing cameras is often overwritten within weeks. Surveillance footage from businesses near the crash site, a gas station on the Katy Freeway, a commercial lot near the Ship Channel, disappears even faster.

The two-year statute of limitations tells you the last possible day to file. It says nothing about when evidence stops being available. Those are two very different deadlines, and confusing them is one of the most damaging mistakes a crash victim can make.

If you were hit by a commercial truck anywhere in the Houston metro, Harris County, Fort Bend, Montgomery, Galveston, Brazoria, the safest move is to talk to an attorney before the six-month ELD window closes. A preservation demand costs you nothing to send. Failing to send one can cost you your strongest evidence.

What to Do Right Now If a Truck Hit You

You don't need to understand the FMCSA's regulatory framework to protect yourself. But there are a few practical steps that matter immediately:

  1. Get the truck's DOT number and carrier name. It's usually on the door or the cab. Photograph it. This identifies the carrier and lets an attorney pull their safety inspection history from the FMCSA database.
  2. Request the police report. Texas Department of Transportation crash reports document the commercial vehicle information and any violations cited at the scene.
  3. Write down everything you remember about the driver's behavior. Was the driver confused? Did they mention being tired? Did they react slowly? This goes in your notes, not on social media.
  4. Don't give a recorded statement to the trucking company's insurer. Their adjuster's job is not to help you. See our post on how adjusters operate after a crash for more on this.
  5. Talk to a truck accident attorney fast. Not because the legal deadline is close, but because the evidence deadline is.
