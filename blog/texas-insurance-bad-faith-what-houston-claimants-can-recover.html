Texas Insurance Bad Faith: When Your Own Insurer Breaks the Law

Texas gives injured claimants two separate legal pathways to hold insurers accountable when they lowball, delay, or deny valid claims. Here's what those pathways look like, and what you can recover beyond your underlying claim.

You paid your premiums for years. You followed the rules. Then you got hurt. maybe on I-610 during rush hour, maybe in a parking lot off Westheimer. and filed a claim with the insurance company you trusted to protect you.

And then the insurer started playing games.

Maybe the adjuster went silent for weeks. Maybe they offered you a fraction of what your medical bills alone cost. Maybe they denied your claim with a vague letter and no real explanation. You're sitting there hurt, confused, and wondering if any of this is even legal.

Here's the short answer: it might not be. Texas law puts real obligations on insurance companies. When they ignore those obligations, you can sue them for it, separately from your underlying injury claim. That's called a bad faith insurance claim, and Texas gives you more than one way to pursue it.

What "Insurance Bad Faith" Actually Means

Every insurance policy is a contract. Built into that contract, under Texas common law, is an implied duty of good faith and fair dealing. The idea is that your insurer can't treat you as an adversary when you file a claim. They agreed to cover you. Now they have to actually do it, fairly.

Bad faith, in plain terms, is when an insurer knows (or should know) that a claim is valid, and either denies it or delays paying it without any reasonable basis for doing so.

That's the common-law standard. Texas courts have recognized it since the 1987 case Arnold v. National County Mutual Fire Insurance Co. It requires proving that the insurer had no reasonable basis for its conduct and that they knew it (or should have).

That standard can be hard to meet. So Texas also created a statutory path that's easier to use.

The Statutory Route: Texas Insurance Code Chapter 541 and the DTPA

Texas Insurance Code Chapter 541 lists specific acts that count as unfair or deceptive insurance practices. You don't have to prove the insurer acted with malicious intent. You just have to show they committed one of the prohibited acts and that it caused you harm.

The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (commonly called the DTPA) also applies to insurance companies in many situations. The DTPA, found at Texas Business & Commerce Code § 17.41 et seq., lets consumers sue businesses that use false, misleading, or deceptive practices. Insurance companies are businesses. Policyholders are consumers. The DTPA gives you another legal tool when your insurer lies to you or misrepresents your coverage.

Both Chapter 541 and the DTPA can allow recovery of attorney's fees and, in cases of knowing violations, additional damages on top of your actual losses.

Specific Acts That Trigger a Bad Faith Claim in Texas

Texas Insurance Code § 541.060 lays out a list of prohibited practices by insurers. These aren't abstract legal concepts. They describe things that actually happen to real policyholders in Harris County and across Texas every week.

Some of the most common violations:

  • Misrepresenting the policy. Telling you that something isn't covered when it actually is, or describing your coverage inaccurately to get you to accept less.
  • Failing to acknowledge your claim promptly. Under Texas Insurance Code § 542.055, your insurer must acknowledge receipt of your claim within 15 days. Many don't.
  • Not accepting or rejecting your claim within a reasonable time. Texas law requires insurers to accept or deny a claim within 15 business days after receiving all items, statements, and forms they asked for, sometimes up to 45 days if they request an extension. Letting your claim sit in limbo indefinitely isn't allowed.
  • Failing to conduct a reasonable investigation. If the insurer decides your claim before they've actually looked at the facts, that's a problem.
  • Offering an unreasonably low settlement. Known in the industry as "lowballing." If the insurer knew your claim was worth significantly more and offered a fraction of it anyway, that can constitute bad faith.
  • Refusing to pay a claim without explaining why. You're entitled to a written explanation that references the specific policy provision they're relying on.

Texas law doesn't just say insurers should treat you fairly. It spells out exactly what "fair" looks like, and gives you a legal remedy when they fall short.

First-Party vs. Third-Party Bad Faith

This distinction matters a lot. It's worth understanding before you decide what to do.

First-party bad faith

This is a dispute between you and your own insurer. The clearest example: you have uninsured motorist (UM) coverage, you get hit by an uninsured driver on the Gulf Freeway, and your own insurance company refuses to pay out what your policy promises. That's first-party bad faith. Your insurer owes you a direct duty of good faith because you're their policyholder.

Third-party bad faith

This comes up when someone else's insurer mistreats you. Say the driver who hit you at Westheimer and Gessner had liability coverage, and their insurer is dragging its feet, misrepresenting the policy, or offering you $800 for a claim that clearly involves serious injuries. Texas recognizes some third-party bad faith claims, though they're more complex because you don't have a direct contract with that insurer.

Your ability to sue a third-party insurer for bad faith is more limited in Texas, but it's not zero. If the at-fault driver assigns their rights to you as part of a settlement, or if you can show the insurer committed an unfair act under Chapter 541, you may still have a path forward.

The strongest bad faith claims in Texas are almost always first-party, against your own insurer.

What You Can Recover in a Texas Bad Faith Case

This is where bad faith law gets meaningful for injured Texans. A successful bad faith claim can get you more than just what you were owed under the policy in the first place.

Actual damages

This covers the harm the bad faith conduct itself caused you, not just the underlying claim amount. If you racked up credit card debt because the insurer delayed your claim for six months and you couldn't pay your medical bills, that interest and those costs may be recoverable. If you had to pay out of pocket for treatment you couldn't postpone, that matters too.

Attorney's fees

Under both Chapter 541 and the Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act (Texas Insurance Code § 542.051 et seq.), you may be able to recover your attorney's fees from the insurer if you prevail. That's significant. It means the insurer potentially pays the cost of your fight against them.

Penalty interest

The Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act adds an 18% per-year interest penalty on claims that weren't paid on time. This accrues on top of the claim amount. When an insurer delays payment for months, that penalty adds up fast. It's meant to deter exactly the kind of delay tactics that adjusters routinely use.

Additional damages for knowing violations

Under Texas Insurance Code § 541.152, if the insurer's violation was committed knowingly, a court can award up to three times the amount of actual damages. This is what's sometimes called "treble damages." It's not automatic. You have to prove the insurer knew what it was doing was wrong. But it's available in serious cases.

Under the DTPA, a similar multiplier applies for knowing or intentional violations.

Common Insurer Tactics That May Cross the Line

Not every frustrating insurance experience rises to the level of bad faith. Insurers can investigate claims, request documentation, and even deny claims, as long as they have a reasonable basis for doing so. The question is always whether there was any legitimate reason for what they did.

That said, some patterns show up repeatedly in bad faith cases handled out of Harris County district courts:

  • Blanket denials with no investigation. The insurer sends a denial letter within days of a claim, before they've even looked at police reports, medical records, or any evidence.
  • Using your recorded statement against you. The adjuster calls you early, gets you to describe your injuries in vague terms before you understand the full extent of them, then points to your own words as a reason to deny or limit your claim. (If you haven't yet, read our post on what to know before giving an insurer a recorded statement.)
  • Disputing causation with no medical basis. The insurer hires a "paper doctor" who reviews records without ever examining you and concludes your injuries aren't related to the accident. This happens in whiplash cases, brain injury cases, and soft-tissue claims constantly.
  • Delaying to pressure a settlement. Some adjusters are trained to let time pass, to let your bills pile up and your stress build, so you'll accept a low offer just to put it behind you.
  • Misrepresenting policy limits. Telling you the at-fault driver only had $30,000 in coverage when they actually had $100,000, hoping you'll settle before you find out.

If the insurer's explanation for denying your claim doesn't make sense, if it seems designed to avoid paying rather than to honestly evaluate your situation, that's worth a second look by an attorney.

Deadlines You Need to Know

Bad faith claims in Texas have their own limitations period. Under Texas Insurance Code § 541.162, you have two years from the date the cause of action accrues to file a bad faith claim. That generally means two years from when the insurer committed the act, not necessarily two years from when your underlying injury claim was resolved.

This matters. If your insurer denied your claim in January, you can't wait three years to challenge it as bad faith and assume you're still within the window. The clock runs from the denial or the bad act itself.

Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003 generally governs two-year statutes of limitations in personal injury cases, and the two-year window on bad faith claims tracks similarly. But the starting point can differ depending on the specific conduct.

If you're already past one year from a denial or a lowball offer, talk to an attorney soon. Don't wait to find out you're time-barred.

What to Do If You Think Your Insurer Is Acting in Bad Faith

Start documenting everything now. The strength of a bad faith claim rests heavily on the paper trail: what the insurer said, when they said it, and how they handled your claim over time.

Keep records of:

  • Every phone call, including date, time, name of the person you spoke with, and what they said
  • Every letter, email, and text from the insurer
  • Every document request they made and when you provided it
  • Every deadline they missed under the Texas Prompt Payment Act
  • Any written denial, including the stated reason

Request everything in writing. If an adjuster tells you something over the phone that surprises you, follow it up with an email: "Just confirming what you told me today, that my claim is denied because of X." That creates a record.

Then talk to an attorney. A bad faith claim isn't something you want to handle on your own. The insurer has lawyers whose entire job is handling these disputes. You should too.

One More Thing: The Stowers Doctrine

If you're the injured party dealing with someone else's insurer, there's another legal concept worth knowing: the Stowers doctrine. Named after a 1929 Texas Supreme Court case, it holds that if a liability insurer fails to settle a claim within policy limits when a reasonable insurer would have done so, the insurer can be held responsible for a judgment that exceeds those limits.

This matters in cases where the at-fault driver's insurer refuses a reasonable settlement offer within their client's policy limits, the case goes to trial, and the jury awards more than the policy covers. The insurer, not just the at-fault driver, may be on the hook for the excess. It's a powerful doctrine that gives liability insurers a real reason to settle reasonable claims rather than gamble with their policyholders' personal assets.

The Stowers doctrine is a third-party bad faith remedy recognized under Texas common law. It gives you real negotiating power when the at-fault driver's insurer is being unreasonable.

The Bottom Line

Insurance companies in Texas aren't free to do whatever they want with your claim. They have legal obligations: hard deadlines, specific duties, and requirements to deal with you honestly. When they don't, Texas law gives you real remedies. Penalty interest, attorney's fees, and in serious cases, additional damages multiplied on top of what you were already owed.

If an adjuster has been giving you the runaround, delaying your claim, offering you a fraction of what you're owed, or denying you without explanation, you may be dealing with more than just a difficult negotiation. You may be dealing with a legal violation.
