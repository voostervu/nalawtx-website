Your Pre-Existing Condition Doesn't Kill Your Claim. Texas Law Says So

Insurance adjusters use your prior back problems, old injuries, and degenerative conditions against you. The Texas eggshell plaintiff rule is the legal doctrine that stops them. Most injured Houstonians have never heard of it.

You had a bad back before the crash. Maybe you'd been treated for degenerative disc disease, a herniated disc from years ago, or an old football injury from high school. Then someone rear-ended you on the I-45 Gulf Freeway, and now your back is significantly worse. You can barely get out of bed, you've missed work, and the pain is affecting every part of your life.

The insurance adjuster calls. They've already pulled your medical records. And they tell you something that sounds very official and very final: "Your injuries are pre-existing. We're not responsible for a condition you already had."

Here's what they're not telling you: that's not how Texas law works.

Texas recognizes a legal doctrine called the eggshell plaintiff rule. It's one of the most important protections available to injured Texans, and one of the most frequently ignored. Understanding it could be the difference between a fair recovery and walking away with nothing.

What the Eggshell Plaintiff Rule Actually Says

The rule gets its name from a simple idea: imagine a person whose skull is as fragile as an eggshell. If someone negligently hits that person and they suffer a catastrophic brain injury, one a healthy person wouldn't have suffered from the same impact, is the at-fault party off the hook because of the victim's fragility?

Texas law says no. Emphatically.

Under the eggshell plaintiff rule, a negligent defendant must take their victim as they find them. If you were already vulnerable, because of a prior injury, a degenerative condition, diabetes, osteoporosis, or anything else, the at-fault party is still responsible for the full harm their negligence caused to you specifically, not to some hypothetical healthy person.

The at-fault driver doesn't get a discount because you weren't in perfect health before they hit you.

Texas courts have applied this principle consistently. The logic is straightforward: a wrongdoer who causes harm doesn't get to escape liability just because the person they harmed happened to be more susceptible to injury. You didn't choose to have a vulnerable spine. The other driver chose to drive negligently.

Why Adjusters Use Pre-Existing Conditions as a Weapon

Insurance adjusters are trained to reduce payouts. That's not cynicism. It's their job. One of the most effective tools they have is your prior medical history, and they will use it against you from the very first phone call.

Here's what that looks like in practice. You're treated at Memorial Hermann or Houston Methodist after the crash. The adjuster requests your complete medical records going back years. They find a note from your primary care doctor three years ago mentioning lower back stiffness, or a 2022 MRI showing mild degenerative disc disease at L4-L5. Suddenly, your entire injury is "pre-existing."

They may use language like:

  • "The imaging shows wear-and-tear that predates the accident."
  • "Your treating physician noted chronic back pain in prior records."
  • "We can only compensate you for injuries directly caused by the accident, not conditions you already had."

All of those statements contain a kernel of truth wrapped around a fundamental lie. Yes, your prior condition exists. No, that doesn't mean the crash didn't significantly worsen it. The worsening is exactly what you're entitled to recover for.

This is deliberate. An unrepresented claimant who doesn't know about the eggshell plaintiff doctrine is far more likely to accept a lowball settlement or give up entirely.

The Distinction That Actually Matters: Aggravation

Texas law draws a clear line between two things that adjusters constantly blur together:

Your pre-existing condition. what you had before the crash. The defendant is not responsible for that.

The aggravation of your pre-existing condition. how the crash made it worse. The defendant is fully responsible for that.

This is the legal concept of aggravation of a pre-existing injury, and it works alongside the eggshell plaintiff rule. Say you had mild degenerative disc disease that was managed with occasional ibuprofen. After a rear-end collision on Westheimer, you now need surgery, epidural steroid injections, and months of physical therapy. The defendant caused that change. You're entitled to recover for the difference between where you were before and where you are now.

Your medical records before the crash are your baseline. Everything worse than that baseline, after the crash, is the defendant's responsibility.

The challenge, and this is where having experienced legal help matters, is proving that difference clearly. That means getting your pre-accident medical records, getting proper post-accident treatment and documentation, and often working with medical experts who can explain to a jury or an adjuster exactly what changed and why.

Common Pre-Existing Conditions That Come Up in Houston Injury Cases

Degenerative disc disease is the most common, but it's far from the only condition adjusters try to weaponize. These are the ones that appear most often in Houston car accident claims:

Degenerative disc disease (DDD). Extremely common in adults over 40. The disc material between vertebrae naturally loses water content and elasticity with age. A crash can accelerate that degeneration dramatically, turn a manageable condition into a debilitating one, or cause a previously stable disc to herniate.

Prior soft tissue injuries. An old whiplash injury from a previous crash, a rotator cuff tear from years ago, or a strained neck from a workplace accident. If the new crash aggravates any of those, you have a claim for the aggravation.

Arthritis. Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis both create joints that are more susceptible to trauma. A crash that would cause minimal joint damage in a young, healthy person can be catastrophic for someone with pre-existing arthritis.

Prior surgeries. If you've had a previous spinal fusion, knee replacement, or other surgery, the affected area may be more fragile and more vulnerable to re-injury in a crash. That vulnerability is yours. The re-injury is the defendant's responsibility.

Neurological conditions. Pre-existing nerve damage, neuropathy, or prior concussions can make a traumatic brain injury more severe. The eggshell plaintiff rule applies here too.

How Texas Courts Handle the Eggshell Plaintiff Rule at Trial

If your case goes to trial at Harris County District Court, or any Texas court, the jury will receive instructions about how to handle pre-existing conditions. Under Texas law, the jury is told to consider what damages the plaintiff suffered as a result of the defendant's negligence, taking the plaintiff as they found them.

Texas Pattern Jury Charge 81.3, which courts use to instruct juries on personal injury damages, asks jurors to award compensation for the injuries the plaintiff actually sustained, not the injuries an average person would have sustained. That's the eggshell plaintiff rule in action inside the courtroom.

What this means practically: a defendant who rear-ends someone on Highway 59 and causes a herniated disc cannot tell the jury "but this person already had a weak spine." The jury awards damages for what actually happened to this actual person.

Texas also follows modified comparative fault. Your recovery is reduced by your share of blame for the accident itself. But having a pre-existing condition isn't "blame." Your prior health status is not negligence on your part. The two are separate questions.

What the Insurance Company Will Try to Do Next

Once adjusters realize you know about the eggshell plaintiff rule, their tactics shift. Instead of flatly denying your claim, they typically move to one of two positions:

Disputing causation. They'll argue the crash wasn't severe enough to have caused your worsening symptoms. Expect them to point to property damage photos, repair estimates, and recorded speed data if available. This is why crash documentation, police reports, and consistent medical treatment starting immediately after the accident all matter.

Apportionment disputes. They'll try to assign a percentage of your current condition to "natural progression" of your pre-existing condition rather than to the crash. This requires medical evidence on your side, specifically a treating physician or expert who can speak to the timeline and explain that the significant worsening followed the crash, not the natural disease progression.

Both of these arguments can be countered with the right medical documentation and legal strategy. But they're much harder to counter if you waited months to get treated, didn't document the accident scene, or gave a recorded statement to the adjuster before speaking with an attorney.

Steps That Protect Your Claim When You Have a Pre-Existing Condition

If you were injured in a Houston crash and you have prior medical history that's relevant, these steps matter more than they do for someone with a completely clean medical record.

Get treated immediately. Same day or the next day. If you've been seen at a facility like CHI St. Luke's or Memorial Hermann, your treatment records establish a clear post-accident baseline. Gaps in treatment are used against you.

Tell your doctors the full story. Don't minimize symptoms, and don't omit your prior history either. Doctors who know your baseline before the crash can document exactly what changed afterward. Honest, complete medical records are your strongest evidence.

Preserve your pre-accident records. If you have prior imaging, treatment notes, or surgical records related to the affected body part, gather them. Your attorney needs to understand your baseline to effectively argue aggravation.

Don't give a recorded statement. The adjuster's recorded statement call is not a formality. It's a data-gathering exercise designed to produce quotes they can use to minimize your claim. Politely decline until you've spoken with an attorney.

Talk to an attorney before accepting any settlement. Once you sign a release, it's over. You can't go back for more money if your condition worsens or you discover additional injuries. An attorney familiar with Texas car accident claims can tell you whether a settlement offer actually accounts for your aggravated condition, or whether it's a lowball number that ignores the eggshell plaintiff doctrine entirely.

The Statute of Limitations Still Applies

One thing the eggshell plaintiff rule does not change: the deadline to file suit in Texas. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, you generally have two years from the date of the accident to file a personal injury lawsuit. That deadline applies whether you have a pre-existing condition or not.

Two years sounds like a long time. It isn't, especially when you're dealing with ongoing medical treatment, insurance negotiations, and the ordinary demands of life after an injury. Cases involving pre-existing conditions often require additional medical expert work, which takes time to arrange. Starting that process early matters.

If you're not sure where your deadline falls, the safest move is to have a free case review sooner rather than later.

You Were Hurt Before. That Doesn't Mean You Don't Deserve to Be Made Whole Now.

Nobody lives in perfect health. Most adults over 40 have something in their medical history, a prior injury, a degenerative condition, an old surgery. That history is part of life, not a legal bar to recovery after someone else's negligence hurts you further.

Texas law recognizes this. The eggshell plaintiff rule exists precisely because the alternative, a legal system where you can only sue if you were perfectly healthy before the crash, would be profoundly unjust. The at-fault driver doesn't get to choose their victims. They take them as they find them.

If an adjuster has told you that your pre-existing condition makes your case worth nothing, you've been misled. That's not an accident or a mistake on their part. It's a tactic. And it works, but only on people who don't know the law.

Now you do.
