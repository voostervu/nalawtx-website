What Every Houston Driver Must Know About Uninsured and Underinsured Motorist Coverage in Texas

Roughly 1 in 7 Texas drivers carries no insurance at all. This post explains what UM/UIM coverage is, why so many Houston drivers accidentally waived it, and what your options are when an uninsured driver hits you.

Picture this: you're stopped at the light at I-10 and Bunker Hill when a driver rear-ends you at highway speed and takes off. Or they stop, hand you an insurance card, and you find out three days later the policy lapsed six months ago. Your car is totaled. Your neck hurts. And the person who did this to you has nothing.

This isn't a hypothetical. It plays out in Harris County courts and hospital emergency rooms constantly. Texas has one of the highest uninsured driver rates in the country. Roughly 14% of drivers on the road have no coverage at all. In a metro area as large as Houston, that means hundreds of thousands of uninsured vehicles sharing the freeways with you every single day.

The insurance product designed to protect you in exactly this situation is called uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage, UM/UIM for short. Most drivers have no idea how it works, and many have quietly signed it away without realizing it. Here's what you need to know.

What Is UM/UIM Coverage, Exactly?

Texas requires every driver to carry a minimum amount of liability insurance. Currently that's $30,000 per person for bodily injury under Texas Transportation Code requirements. That's the other driver's policy. It pays you when the other driver is at fault.

But what happens when the at-fault driver has no policy at all? Or their policy limit is $30,000 and your hospital bill from Memorial Hermann is $85,000? That's where UM/UIM coverage steps in.

Uninsured motorist (UM) coverage pays your medical bills, lost wages, and other damages when the at-fault driver has zero insurance, including hit-and-run crashes where the driver flees and can't be identified.

Underinsured motorist (UIM) coverage kicks in when the at-fault driver has insurance, but their policy limit is too low to cover your actual losses. If they carry $30,000 in liability and your damages are $120,000, UIM coverage can bridge that gap.

Both coverages come from your own insurance company. You're essentially buying a backstop policy that protects you from other people's bad decisions.

Texas Law Requires Your Insurer to Offer It. Not to Give It to You

Here's where a lot of Houston drivers get blindsided. Under Texas Insurance Code § 1952.101, every auto insurer must offer UM/UIM coverage to every Texas policyholder. But the law doesn't require you to accept it.

To decline UM/UIM coverage, you have to sign a written rejection. That rejection form is often buried in a stack of paperwork when you're signing up for a new policy. Sometimes it's online, where it's one checkbox among dozens. Many drivers check the box without understanding they're giving up meaningful protection.

If you've never specifically thought about UM/UIM coverage, there's a real chance you declined it without knowing. Pull up your declarations page right now and look. If you don't see "UM" or "UIM" listed, you probably don't have it.

Insurers aren't required to explain what you're waiving. They're required to offer it and get your signature. That's it. The result: countless Houston drivers are out there every day believing they're covered, and they're not.

Why This Matters More in Houston Than Almost Anywhere Else

Houston already carries the highest full-coverage auto insurance rates of any major Texas city. That makes cost pressure real. People look for ways to trim premiums, and UM/UIM is an easy target because the benefit isn't obvious until you actually need it.

At the same time, Houston's road conditions make that coverage more valuable here than most places. The city logged 301 traffic fatalities in 2024, the highest annual total on record. The corridors along US-59, I-45 South toward Galveston, and the I-610 loop see some of the state's highest crash densities. Hit-and-run crashes are a particular problem too: Harris County routinely ranks among Texas counties with the most hit-and-run incidents reported to TxDOT.

When a driver who hits you on the Southwest Freeway has no insurance and no assets, your UM coverage may be the only real source of recovery available to you. Without it, you're left pursuing a civil judgment against someone who likely has nothing collectible. That process costs time and money and often yields nothing.

What UM/UIM Coverage Actually Pays For

People often assume UM/UIM is just for medical bills. It's more than that. Depending on your policy, UM/UIM coverage can compensate you for:

  • Medical expenses. emergency room visits, surgery, physical therapy, follow-up care
  • Lost wages. income you couldn't earn while recovering
  • Pain and suffering. compensation for the physical and emotional toll of the injury
  • Future medical costs. if your injuries require ongoing treatment
  • Property damage. some policies include uninsured motorist property damage (UMPD) as a separate coverage

The coverage limits you carry determine the ceiling. If you bought a policy with $30,000 in UM coverage and your losses are $90,000, you're still exposed for the difference. This is why attorneys generally recommend purchasing UM/UIM limits that match or exceed your liability limits.

Hit-and-Run Crashes: A Special Situation

Hit-and-run accidents are one of the most frustrating situations an injury victim faces. The driver who hurt you is gone. There may be no witnesses. Police may not find them.

Your UM coverage is specifically designed for this scenario. Under Texas law, a hit-and-run driver qualifies as an "uninsured motorist," meaning your own UM policy can respond even when you never identify who hit you.

There is one important catch. Most Texas UM policies require that the hit-and-run vehicle made physical contact with your vehicle. If someone cut you off and ran you into a barrier without touching your car, some insurers will dispute the claim. This is a known area of policy interpretation, and it's worth understanding before you need it.

If you were hit and the driver fled, call 911 immediately and get a police report, even if the damage looks minor. That report is often essential for a UM claim. Without it, your insurer has grounds to push back.

Witnesses matter too. If anyone saw the crash, get their name and phone number before they leave the scene. Dashcam footage, nearby surveillance cameras, and traffic cameras along Houston's managed lanes can all support your version of events.

How UM/UIM Claims Actually Work. And Why They're Not Simple

Filing a UM/UIM claim means filing against your own insurer. That might sound straightforward. It often isn't.

Your insurance company has a financial interest in paying you as little as possible on a UM/UIM claim, just like any other insurer would. They may dispute the severity of your injuries, question whether the accident caused them, or argue that your medical treatment was excessive. They have adjusters and attorneys working these files. People whose job is claim management, not your recovery.

Texas does impose some protections. Under Texas Insurance Code § 542, insurers must acknowledge your claim promptly, investigate it in good faith, and pay valid claims within specific timeframes. Violations can result in penalty interest and attorney's fees under what's commonly called the Texas Prompt Payment of Claims Act. But those protections only help if you know to invoke them.

A few things UM/UIM claimants routinely underestimate:

  • The recorded statement request. Your insurer may call shortly after the crash and ask for a recorded statement. You're generally not required to give one immediately, and what you say can be used to limit your claim. Talk to an attorney first if your injuries are serious.
  • The early settlement offer. Some insurers move fast with a low offer before you know the full extent of your injuries. Accepting it typically closes your claim permanently.
  • The two-year deadline. Under Texas Civil Practice & Remedies Code § 16.003, personal injury claims in Texas generally must be filed within two years of the date of the accident. Miss that window and your claim is gone, including your UM/UIM claim.

What to Do Right After an Accident With an Uninsured Driver

The steps you take in the first 48 hours shape everything that comes after. Here's what matters most:

  1. Call 911. Get a police report. This is non-negotiable if you think the other driver may be uninsured or if they flee the scene.
  2. See a doctor, that day or the next. Even if you feel okay, get evaluated. Soft tissue injuries from crashes often don't peak for 24-72 hours. A gap in medical treatment gives insurers ammunition to deny your claim.
  3. Document everything. Photos of the scene, your vehicle, your injuries. Screenshot the other driver's insurance card before they drive away. Note the plate number.
  4. Notify your own insurance company. You're required to report the accident under your policy terms. Do it, but stick to basic facts. You don't need to characterize fault or your injuries in detail at this stage.
  5. Don't sign anything from an adjuster until you understand what you're agreeing to.

If your injuries are significant, anything beyond minor soreness, talk with a Houston car accident attorney before giving a recorded statement to your own insurer. This isn't paranoia. It's practical. Once you've signed a release or given a statement that minimizes your injuries, it's very difficult to undo.

Stacking: Can You Use Multiple UM/UIM Policies?

Texas allows something called "stacking" in limited circumstances, combining the UM/UIM limits from multiple policies to cover your losses. For example, if you have two vehicles on separate policies, you may be able to stack those coverages if one policy doesn't prohibit it.

This is genuinely complicated. Some policies have anti-stacking provisions. How stacking interacts with subrogation rights (the insurer's right to recover what it paid you from a third party) adds another layer. If you're in a situation where your losses may exceed your single-policy UM limit, an attorney can help you figure out whether stacking is available and what it would mean for your claim.

Should You Buy More UM/UIM Coverage?

If you don't currently have UM/UIM on your policy, the honest answer is: you should seriously consider adding it. The premium difference is usually modest, often a few dollars a month, relative to the protection it provides.

At minimum, match your UM/UIM limits to your liability limits. If you carry $100,000 per person in liability, carry at least $100,000 in UM/UIM. Texas allows you to purchase UM/UIM limits up to the amount of your liability coverage.

Given what Houston roads look like, the uninsured driver rate, the crash volume, the hit-and-run problem, UM/UIM coverage is one of the few insurance products that actually earns its cost on a regular basis. Talk to your insurance agent about your current limits and get the specifics in writing.
